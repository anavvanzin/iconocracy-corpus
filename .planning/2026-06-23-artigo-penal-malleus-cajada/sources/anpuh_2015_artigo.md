## Executive Summary
Araújo provides a rigorous historical reconstruction of the case of Maria Gonçalves Cajada, a Portuguese *cristã-velha* (old Christian) born in Estremoz, who was degredada (exiled) to Pernambuco and later moved to Salvador, Bahia, where she was arrested during Heitor Furtado de Mendonça’s First Visitation of the Holy Office (1591–1593). Arrested under the colorful vernacular nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" ("Her tail burns"), her process is analysed as a key intersection between the metropolitan, Latinate demonology of the *Malleus Maleficarum* and the local, vernacular practices of magic and negotiation in the 16th-century Portuguese colony.

## Methodological Framework

### 1. The Indiciary Paradigm (Carlo Ginzburg)
- Araújo employs Ginzburg's *paradigma indiciário* (indiciary paradigm) to read the inquisitorial documents "against the grain."
- Inquisitorial records are not neutral reflections of truth but the product of a highly unequal power dynamic (the Holy Office interrogating a terrified, isolated accused).
- The historian must look for clues, marginal details, slips of the tongue, and discrepancies in the transcripts to reconstruct the genuine social reality and agency of the accused subaltern.
- Quoting Ginzburg:
  > *"Devem ser lidos como o produto de uma interrelação especial, em que há um desequilíbrio total das partes nela envolvidas..."*

### 2. Marc Bloch's Critical Reading
- Araújo cites Marc Bloch to emphasize that the historian should value what the document accidentally reveals or "lets us understand" without meaning to state it explicitly.

## Key Historical Findings

### 1. The Context of the First Visitation in Bahia (1591–1593)
- Led by Visitador Heitor Furtado de Mendonça.
- The reading of the "Edital da Fé" and the proclamation of the "Período da Graça" (30-day grace period) created a pervasive atmosphere of fear, encouraging self-denunciation and neighborhood delations.
- While the only permanent colonial tribunal was in Goa, the Brazilian colonies were policed through periodic visitations, bishops, and local commissioners, creating a powerful "transplant of metropolitan suspicion."

### 2. Maria Gonçalves Cajada, "Arde-lhe-o-rabo"
- Natural of Estremoz, Portugal, *cristã-velha*.
- Already had a criminal record before arriving in Bahia: she had been exiled to Pernambuco for six years for burning down houses and assaulting a judge, performing a public penance with a *carocha* (infamous hat) in front of a church.
- Lived in Bahia for over ten years without a fixed address, practicing professional magic for remuneration (amansar maridos, curar doenças, adivinhações).
- The nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" was a popular tropo of suspicion coined by her neighbors. Araújo reads this popular label as a **vernacular translation** of the academic, theological category of *maleficium* described in the *Malleus Maleficarum*.

### 3. The Procedural "Technology of the Corpo Confitente" in Practice
- Cajada's process (proc. 10748) illustrates the standard sequence of inquisitorial subjection:
  1. **Secrecy:** Total isolation in the cells of the visitador, preventing contact with neighbors and sanitizing the narrative.
  2. **Tortura Regulada (Regulated Torture):** Regulated strictly by inquisitorial rules (presence of a doctor, scribe, and specific physical limits) but designed to reshape the accused's subjectivity.
  3. **Confissão:** Under pressure and torture, Cajada confessed to demonic pacts, invocation of devils, and making potions.
  4. **Auto de Fé (Auto-da-fé):** Her public sentence was read at a dramatic, highly visual ceremony, publicly validating the "truth" manufactured in secrecy.

## Utility for the Article's Argument
1. **The Role of the Nickname:** "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" serves as a perfect example of a popular, vernacular translation of *maleficium*.
2. **Cajada as Pivô Documental:** The paper supports treating Cajada as a *pivot* rather than an isolated study of case. She is the physical point of contact where metropolitan demonology (Seção 1) and procedural technologies (Seção 2) are enacted upon a colonial body, paving the way for the complex, hybrid transimperial circulations of Seção 4.
