## Executive Summary
This volume is the definitive historiographical study of the trial, exile, and social context of Maria Gonçalves Cajada, known as "Arde-lhe-o-rabo." Cruz de Araújo combines rigorous archival transcription with gender history, microhistory, and historical-sociological analysis of the 16th-century Portuguese Empire. The book demonstrates how Cajada, an illiterate *cristã-velha* from Estremoz, utilized her marginalized agency to survive through professional magical practices in Pernambuco and Bahia, and how her gendered, vocal resistance ultimately led her neighbors to translate her actions into the vernacular nickname of "Arde-lhe-o-rabo"—which was eventually co-opted and demonized by the Inquisitorial Visitador Heitor Furtado de Mendonça.

## Key Historiographical Insights and Excerpts

### 1. Maria Gonçalves Cajada’s Transatlantic Exile Path
- Natural of Estremoz, Portugal, *cristã-velha*, married to a sailor.
- **Exile 1:** Sentenced to 6 years of exile (degredo) to Pernambuco, Brazil, for burning down a house and physically assaulting a local judge. She performed a public penance wearing a *carocha* (infamous pointed hat) in Estremoz.
- **Exile 2:** After Pernambuco, she moved to Salvador, Bahia (around 1578), where she lived for over 10 years without a fixed address, renting rooms and traveling.
- **Arrest & Shipping:** Arrested during the First Visitation (1591–1593) by Heitor Furtado de Mendonça, her assets were seized, and she was shipped in chains to the Inquisition prison in Lisbon (Torre do Tombo) for formal interrogation.

### 2. The Nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" as Vernacular *Maleficium*
- Cruz de Araújo discusses how the nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" (lit. "Her tail burns") was coined by her neighbors.
- It was a popular, somatic tropo of suspicion. It represented the community's vernacular way of labeling her as a dangerous, volatile, and sexually transgressive agent.
- When the Visitation arrived, the neighbors translated this local, somatic label into formal depositions of *feitiçaria* (witchcraft). The Inquisitorial Visitador, in turn, translated this vernacular "burning tail" into the formal, Latinate categories of *maleficium* and demonic pacts influenced by the *Malleus Maleficarum*.

### 3. The Structural Step-by-Step of Trial 10748
- **Denunciations:** Standard neighbor complaints (making potions, predicting the future, "taming" violent husbands, invoking magical entities for money).
- **Isolation:** Held in strict secrecy (*segredo*) to ensure the Inquisition controlled the narrative of her "crimes."
- **Torture:** Subjected to formal torture sessions in Lisbon, during which she confessed to invoking "the devil under the bed" and making potions with bones and herbs.
- **The Verdict:** Sentenced to perform a public penance at an *auto-da-fé* in Lisbon, followed by continued exile. The tribunal’s sentence noted, in a striking moment of institutional self-doubt: *"Parece que tudo são embustes e enganos as culpas desta ré."* ("It seems that all the charges of this defendant are but tricks and deceits.")

## Utility for the Article's Core Argument
1. **The Core Textual Evidence:** Cruz de Araújo’s book provides the exact, physical details of Processo 10748, including the crucial sentence showing the Inquisition's self-doubt (*"tudo são embustes..."*), which forms the core of Seção 3.
2. **Cajada as Pivot:** It supports treating Cajada as a *pivot* rather than an isolated study of case. Her life path (Estremoz → Pernambuco → Bahia → Lisbon) is a literal physical map of the transatlantic, transimperial circulation of penal subjection analyzed in Seção 4.
