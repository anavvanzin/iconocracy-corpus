## Executive Summary
Brian Levack’s seminal work provides a comparative structural history of the witch-hunts across early modern Europe. In Chapter 9, Levack focuses on the Iberian world (Portugal, Spain, and their overseas empires), arguing that their pattern of persecution represents a striking deviation from the massive witch-panics of central and northern Europe. Persecution in the Iberian world was almost exclusively monopolized by the centralized Inquisition rather than local secular courts. This institutional monopoly resulted in far lower execution rates, a strict adherence to judicial procedures, and a distinct focus on specific, localized *maleficium* (harmful magic/healer practices) rather than the grand theological conspiracy of the witches' Sabbat.

---

## Key Concepts and Analytical Arguments

### 1. The Iberian Inquisitorial Monopoly
- In Germany, France, and Switzerland, witch-hunting was largely conducted by local secular judges acting under pressure from local communities, which often led to runaway panics and thousands of executions.
- In Portugal and Spain, the Holy Office (established in 1478 in Spain, 1536 in Portugal) asserted an early and absolute monopoly over the crime of witchcraft.
- The Inquisitions were highly centralized, bureaucratic institutions answerable directly to the crown (the Consejo de la Suprema in Spain, the Conselho Geral in Portugal). Local tribunals could not execute an accused witch without direct confirmation from the central metropolitan council. This bureaucratic drag-chain acted as a powerful brake on mass panic.

### 2. Focus on *Maleficium* and Healing Over the Sabbat
- The Iberian populations (especially in Portugal and its colonies) practiced a wide range of vernacular magic: curandeirismo (healing), benzeduras (blessings), and small-scale love magic (feitiçaria/hechicería).
- The *Malleus Maleficarum* (1487) did circulate in Iberia through Latin and Spanish translations, and was incorporated into inquisitorial procedural guides. However, Iberian inquisitors read the *Malleus* through their own legal-procedural filter.
- They prioritized concrete evidence of physical harm (*maleficium*) or heresy (apostasy, pacts with the devil) over fantasies of collective nocturnal flight and sexual Sabbat. This "rationalizing" legal posture prevented the hysterical spirals seen in the Protestant north.

### 3. Centralized Institutional "Transplant"
- Levack defines the expansion of inquisitorial authority to the colonies (such as Brazil, Angola, and Goa) as a process of "institutional transplantation."
- Instead of setting up permanent local tribunals in Brazil (which only occurred in Goa), the Portuguese Inquisition operated dynamically through:
  - Sporadic, authorized "Visitations" (such as the 1591–1593 Visitation to Bahia).
  - Local networks of "Comissários" (commissioners) and "Familiares" (lay assistants).
  - Centralizing the physical processes: complex or high-profile colonial defendants (like Maria Gonçalves Cajada) were shipped back to Lisbon for interrogation, torture, and sentencing.

## Analytical Utility for the Article's Core Argument
1. **Institutions over Ideas:** Levack’s comparative model supports the article's division of labor: the *Malleus* provides the gendered discourse (Seção 1), but the Portuguese Inquisition provides the specific procedural machinery—the "technology of the corpo confitente" (Seção 2)—that converts discourse into practice.
2. **The Nature of Colonial Prosecution:** It explains why the prosecution of witchcraft in Brazil and other colonies remained centralized in Lisbon, showing that the transatlantic circulation of the type penal was a highly institutional, imperial project rather than a vague cultural flow (Seção 4).
