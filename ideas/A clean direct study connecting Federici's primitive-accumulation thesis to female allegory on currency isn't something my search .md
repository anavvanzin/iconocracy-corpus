A clean direct study connecting Federici's primitive-accumulation thesis to female allegory on currency isn't something my search surfaced — the question sits in the gap between two literatures that rarely cite each other. But the building blocks for the argument are well established, and they line up suggestively. The synthesis below is mine; the load-bearing pieces are sourced.

**The two processes are contemporaneous, not just metaphorically related.** Federici's argument in *Caliban and the Witch* is that the 15th–17th-century witch hunts, enclosures of the commons, and criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy were not pre-modern residue but the constitutive violence of capitalist primitive accumulation — the female body itself was enclosed, expropriated, and converted into an unwaged reservoir of reproductive labour [^1]. The same long early-modern arc that drove this enclosure is when the allegorical female form is consolidated as the privileged carrier of state and civic abstraction. Antoine de Baecque shows that by the late 18th century allegory had become the mode "most suited to the incarnation of political sovereignty," and that "the goddesses Truth, Liberty, Abundance or Justice" were the figures through which men of state, artists and citizens "acceded to abstract thought via metaphor" [^2]. Marina Warner's *Monuments and Maidens* names the structural puzzle directly: liberty, justice, wisdom and charity are personified in the female form precisely against, and in tension with, women's "historic" position [^3].

Read together, Warner and Federici describe two faces of one operation. Federici's process strips concrete women of legal, economic and bodily standing — expelled from guilds and waged work, criminalised as witches when autonomous, fixed as unpaid reproducers in the household [^1]. Warner's process installs the abstracted Woman on the pediment, the seal, the coin: the female form is conscriptable for civic universals exactly because, in patriarchal society, women do not signify themselves [^3]. Allegorical Woman can stand for the *res publica* in a way allegorical Man cannot, because the female sign has been emptied of the political-economic agency that would otherwise tie it to a particular interest.

**Currency is where this becomes especially legible.** Virginia Hewitt's survey of two centuries of women on paper money is blunt about the function: "Proud Britannias and crowned monarchs, glamour girls and cotton-pickers have all been summoned to proclaim the wealth of a bank, the power of a state or the glory of a nation" — images "carefully chosen" that both reflect and reinforce "our conflicting perceptions of women and their place in society" [^4]. The conflict Hewitt names is Federici's and Warner's: the abstract female on the note co-exists with — and depends on — the disciplined female outside it. A recent semiotic study of Indonesia's 2022 banknote series puts the gap in present-tense form: on the surface the notes look like "appreciation for women," but the analysis finds them still "lack a sense of justice towards women" [^5]. The female image on currency is the form respect takes once substantive standing has been withheld.

**There is also a structural argument that sharpens this.** Jean-Joseph Goux's account of "general equivalents," picked up by Diane Cady, reads money as a *masculine* equivalent — the active, stable, idealised measure — against which "secondary signs" are placed in a passive, measurable, exchangeable position, and Goux traces this hierarchy back to Aristotelian form/matter gendering: men provide form, women the passive matter to be imprinted [^6]. If money is the masculine general equivalent and woman the feminine secondary sign, then engraving the allegorical female onto the coin is not a contradiction — it is the pairing made visible. The note carries the active, measuring authority; the figure on it carries the passive, idealised matter to be measured. Federici describes the same active/passive split as the lived outcome of primitive accumulation: men's labour rendered visible as waged value, women's labour rendered invisible as nature/reproduction [^1]. Nicky Marsh extends this into the symbolic register, reading the recurring "virgin and whore" dyad and the "policing of [maternity's] limits" as cultural work that money's symbolism performs on female sexual reproduction [^7].

**A few honest caveats.**

- The directly cited Federici material in my results is mostly review and reception literature rather than *Caliban and the Witch* itself; I'm relying on the enclosure-of-the-body thesis as it is restated by Quiroga Díaz and Gago [^1], which is a faithful but secondary articulation.
- The neat chronological alignment (witch hunts → Marianne/Britannia/Justitia on coinage) is suggestive but somewhat compressed. Allegorical female personifications of states predate primitive accumulation (Roma, Athena, medieval Ecclesia), and their mass deployment on *paper currency* is largely a 19th–20th-century phenomenon — well after Federici's core period. The argument is therefore less "these things happened simultaneously" and more "the early-modern enclosure produced the gendered subject-form that 19th-century state iconography and central-bank design then drew on freely."
- I did not find a paper that explicitly puts Federici and Warner (or Federici and currency iconography) in the same frame. That would be a real gap worth a piece of writing.
