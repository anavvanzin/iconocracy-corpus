\# The Coin and the Witch: A Federician Iconography of Female Allegory

\#\# Essay outline

\#\# Thesis

Modern state iconography of female allegory — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia, Lady Liberty, the female figures on coinage — is not a transhistorical cultural inheritance to be read symbolically. It is an iconographic \*output\* of primitive accumulation. The same early-modern process that Silvia Federici describes as the enclosure of the commons and the disciplining of women's bodies produced the gendered subject-form that 18th–19th-century states then engraved onto their seals, pediments, and money. Marina Warner's iconography named the pattern; Federici's political economy supplies the mechanism. Reading them together — against the grain of both — turns the abstract civic Maiden into the visible face of an invisible history: the post-mortem mask of the witch, the receipt for an expropriation, stamped on the instrument of a general equivalent.

\---

\#\# I. The problem: two literatures that rarely cite each other

\- Federici's \*Caliban and the Witch\* frames the 15th–17th-century witch hunts, enclosures, and criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy as the constitutive violence of capitalist primitive accumulation — the female body itself enclosed, expropriated, converted into an unwaged reservoir of reproductive labour \[^1\].  
\- Warner's \*Monuments and Maidens\* documents the structural puzzle: liberty, justice, wisdom, and charity are personified as female precisely against, and in tension with, women's historic position \[^2\].  
\- Antoine de Baecque names the late-18th-century consolidation: allegory becomes the mode "most suited to the incarnation of political sovereignty," and Truth, Liberty, Abundance, and Justice are the figures through which the state, artists, and citizens "accede to abstract thought via metaphor" \[^3\].  
\- The two literatures describe two faces of one operation. The essay's move is to fuse them.

\#\# II. Warner and Federici: where they actually disagree

\- \*\*Object of analysis.\*\* Warner reads representations of women; Federici reads what was done to women. Warner's unit is the image (Virgin, Joan of Arc, Justitia, Britannia) \[^2\]; Federici's is the labouring, reproducing body under a regime of "state control of \[women's\] sexuality, reproduction, and political power" \[^4\].  
\- \*\*Periodisation.\*\* Warner treats feminine allegory as a transhistorical Western inheritance \[^5\]. Federici insists on a datable rupture: the modern gender order was \*produced\* by early-modern accumulation, not inherited \[^4\]\[^6\].  
\- \*\*Causal direction.\*\* Warner runs from patriarchy to imagery; Federici runs from capital, through violence, to the gendered subject. Sokolofsky's Marx-Foucault reading names this as "the dialectical double helix of violence and compulsion" \[^7\].  
\- \*\*Class.\*\* Warner's archive is high-cultural; Federici's protagonists are peasant women, midwives, healers \[^6\].  
\- \*\*Politics.\*\* Warner is demystificatory and ambivalent about figures like the Virgin \[^8\]; Federici is reconstructive and materialist — reclaim the commons, revalue reproductive labour, rehabilitate the witch \[^4\]\[^9\].  
\- \*\*The live disagreement.\*\* Warner's framework reads allegorical Woman as polysemic. Federici's framework treats that polysemy as ideological — the residue of having abstracted from the material process that produced the sign.

\#\# III. The structural argument: money as masculine equivalent, allegory as feminine matter

\- Goux, taken up by Cady, reads money as the \*masculine\* general equivalent — active, stable, idealised measure — against which "secondary signs" occupy the passive, measurable, exchangeable position. The hierarchy runs back to Aristotelian form/matter gendering: men provide form, women the passive matter to be imprinted \[^10\].  
\- Nicky Marsh extends this into the symbolic register — the recurring virgin/whore dyad and the "policing of maternity's limits" is cultural work money's symbolism performs on female sexual reproduction \[^11\].  
\- Currency is where the pairing becomes legible. Hewitt's blunt summary of two centuries of women on paper money: images "carefully chosen" to "proclaim the wealth of a bank, the power of a state or the glory of a nation," that both reflect and reinforce "our conflicting perceptions of women" \[^12\].  
\- A present-tense case: semiotic analysis of Indonesia's 2022 banknote series finds the notes look like "appreciation for women" but "lack a sense of justice towards women" \[^13\]. The female image on currency is the form respect takes once substantive standing has been withheld.

\#\# IV. Reading Marianne — the citizen-woman who is not a citizen

\- \*\*The exclusion that makes the sign available.\*\* The Republic that puts Marianne on its seal in 1792 executes Olympe de Gouges in 1793 for proposing that the rights of man apply to women — her \*Déclaration des droits de la femme\* (1791) is answered with the guillotine \[^14\]. Popular Revolutionary print culture actually moves "from feminine to masculine symbolism" as the political-female is silenced \[^15\].  
\- \*\*Cleansed of every dangerous attribute.\*\* Marianne "represented the virtues so desired by the new order: the transcendence of localism, superstition, and particularity… She belonged to no group, to no particular place" \[^16\]. Federici's witch hunt is the extinction, in concrete peasant women, of exactly those attributes.  
\- \*\*The politicised breast.\*\* Delacroix's \*Liberty Leading the People\* stages the bared breast as republican commonplace \[^17\]. Federici's frame reads this as the state annexing, as its insignia, the bodily surface it has just been criminalising in peasant women.  
\- \*\*The material other side.\*\* Revolutionary land reform privatises the commons; Humphries shows that the loss of common rights, "primarily exploited" by women and children, drove women into wage dependency on male earners \[^18\]. Marianne's "liberty" circulates as iconography of freedom precisely as women's economic standing is hollowed out.  
\- \*\*The pairing on the coin.\*\* Allegory as sovereignty's form \[^19\], Marianne as passive matter stamped by the active masculine measure \[^10\]. By 1804 (the Napoleonic Code) women are explicitly outside citizenship.

\#\# V. Reading Britannia — the conquered woman who becomes the conqueror's brand

\- \*\*Origin as conquest image.\*\* Britannia begins on the reverse of Hadrian's coins as the personification of "the conquered island"; the British state revives her, models her on Athena (helmet, shield), and gives her Neptune's trident \[^20\]. The female sign that stamps British money was, in its first life, the figure of an \*expropriated\* territory.  
\- \*\*Enclosure and empire on one coin.\*\* Britannia's mass appearance on British coinage (Charles II's halfpenny 1672; the great recoinage of 1797\) tracks parliamentary enclosure (1750–1830), the East India Company, and the Atlantic slave system. The coin is the instrument of the new general equivalent; the disciplined female form is what the instrument bears.  
\- \*\*The manufactured femininity, enthroned.\*\* Britannia "typically conveys self-possession, self-discipline, and superior calm: she is rarely depicted as agitated or over-indulging" \[^20\]. Almost line-for-line the femininity Federici argues the witch hunt manufactured: "sexless, obedient, submissive, and subordinate" \[^21\].  
\- \*\*The colonial inflection.\*\* Britannia is "Mother Country" — female personifications of the settler colonies were "ideal images for attracting both male and female emigrants" and recruiting women as "imperial agents for preventing racial and moral decline" \[^22\]. On the ground, actual reproductive labour is outsourced to colonised women — \*amah\*, \*ayah\*, wet-nurse \[^23\]. Britannia is the visible face of an invisible reproductive division of labour structured by race and empire.

\#\# VI. Reading Justitia — the blindfolded judge whose court tried the witches

\- \*\*A datable icon inside a long genealogy.\*\* Justitia is medieval and Roman in origin, but the \*modern\* iconographic form — female judge with scales, sword, blindfold — is consolidated inside the witch-hunt window. The earliest known blindfolded Justitia is a satirical woodcut in Sebastian Brant's \*Ship of Fools\* (1494), where the blindfold is a \*negative\* attribute for foolish, arbitrary justice \[^24\].  
\- \*\*The valence flip.\*\* Over the 15th–17th centuries the blindfold reverses meaning, from mark of derision to emblem of impartiality \[^25\]. Prosperi's \*Justice Blindfolded\* traces the flip across the "Catholic Christendom, 1300–1700" window \[^26\] — the same window as the witch trials.  
\- \*\*The courtroom made the witch.\*\* Karlsen shows the accused in colonial New England were disproportionately women who had inherited property outside male lines — witchcraft as "social construction" rather than description of behaviour \[^27\]. Federici's argument: witch-hunting was "indivisible from men's fear of women's knowledge, labor, and power," and drove "the systematic dispossession, torture, and extermination of countless women who resisted the rise of capitalism" \[^28\].  
\- \*\*The Federician reading.\*\* The female sign of impartial justice functions by \*not seeing\* the sex/class/race asymmetry of who is in the dock. Capers presses the point into the American register: what does it mean for Justitia to be blind "in a racialized society where color is so determinative"? \[^29\].

\#\# VII. Reading Columbia and Lady Liberty — the settler mother, and the Indian Princess she replaced

\- \*\*The substitution.\*\* 16th–17th-century European allegories of America personified the continent as an Indian Queen or Princess, often bare-breasted and feathered \[^30\]. Fleming's classic study traces the arc 1783–1815: the American image moves "from Indian Princess to Greek Goddess," Columbia proliferating "to represent a grateful nation on Congressional medals, a dignified nation on official coins" \[^31\]. Not a stylistic choice — the substitution tracks the underlying accumulation.  
\- \*\*Property and personhood.\*\* Colonial and early-national property law "assumes women would remain dependent and subservient after marriage" \[^32\]. Salem (1692) sits in the same arc, targeting women whose property autonomy exceeded patriarchal norms \[^27\]. The early Republic refuses to construe married women as autonomous citizens even when it enforces their obligations \[^33\]. "Republican Motherhood" is introduced "as a means to alleviate women's elated political interests, and thus reduce women's role into motherhood and confine them to their home" \[^34\].  
\- \*\*The racial content.\*\* After the 1807/1808 slave-trade bans, "cultural producers began bifurcating constitutional from personal freedom in their iterations of Columbia"; postabolition Columbia is staged in a Temple of Liberty from which black figures are erased — "an erasure that reinforced whiteness as the defining qualification for American citizenship" \[^35\].  
\- \*\*Later confirmations.\*\* Sund on the 1893 Columbian Exposition finds Columbia "perpetuating the use of the female form as depersonalized vessel of allegory and passive reflector of fantasy" against contemporaneous feminist oratory \[^36\]. Marusek on post-1999 US quarters: coinage "legally manipulate\[s\] our American cultural historical recollection," making "racial violence, imperialist conquest, and the elimination of native peoples… dim against picturesque images" \[^37\].

\#\# VIII. Who has tried to bridge Warner and Federici

\- \*\*Gen Doy, \*Seeing and Consciousness\* (1995).\*\* Programmatic Marxist-feminist iconography of women from the French Revolution forward — the female sign as class artefact, not just gendered \[^38\]\[^39\].  
\- \*\*Kozomora, "To Conjure Her" (2022).\*\* Reads Selman and Kamerić through Federici; the witch as both Federician body and Warnerian "metaphoric marker of the national body, a symbolic battlefield" \[^21\].  
\- \*\*The "witching of art history" current.\*\* Deepwell (\*Misogyny: Witches and Wicked Bodies\*) \[^40\], Naessens on the W.I.T.C.H./Witch Bloc lineage as "feminist, ecologist, anti-capitalist" \[^41\], Kokkinen framing "witching" as political method for art history \[^42\], Turley-Sonne's \*Art & Magic\* \[^43\]. Federici cited \*for the witch\*, not for the iconographic argument.  
\- \*\*Mansoor, "Militant Landscape" (2021).\*\* Turner read against Marx on primitive accumulation; contemporary practice as "counter-figuration" \[^44\].  
\- \*\*Vishmidt, "Speaking of Bodies" (2017).\*\* "What form of social relation makes such a thing as 'the body' not just legible but a departure point for any political subjectivation?" — refuses both Warnerian culturalism and pre-political readings of Federici's body \[^45\].  
\- \*\*Daskalaki (2021).\*\* Federici's witch as an organising figure for social-reproduction practice \[^46\].  
\- \*\*Zika, \*The Appearance of Witchcraft\* (2007).\*\* The 15th–16th-century explosion of witch \*imagery\* was not decoration of the witch hunt but constitutive of it — print and visual culture fused demonological theory with "male fantasies about female power" and made persecution thinkable \[^47\]. Art history materialising Warner's territory back into Federici's period.

\#\# IX. Where the bridge is still missing

\- A sustained materialist iconography of female \*civic allegory\* proper — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia — read through Federici's account of primitive accumulation. Doy comes close for revolutionary France; Kozomora for post-Yugoslav nation-bodies; the "witching" literature for the witch. The cleanly Warnerian object — the abstract civic Maiden — has not received a sustained Federician treatment.  
\- Goux–Cady's general-equivalents argument \[^10\] is the closest theoretical scaffolding; no one has yet pulled it through to a developed reading of specific icons.

\#\# X. Conclusion — the four figures as one template

The essay's payoff: the four figures stop looking like distinct icons and start looking like variations on a template. Each pairs a specific accumulation with a specific disciplined femininity:

\- \*\*Marianne / France / enclosure \+ republican civic exclusion\*\* → the exposed insurrectionary maiden.  
\- \*\*Britannia / Britain / colonial and imperial expansion\*\* → the armoured imperial matron.  
\- \*\*Justitia / the state form itself / criminalisation of women\*\* → the blindfolded judge.  
\- \*\*Columbia / settler America / indigenous dispossession and racialised slavery\*\* → the classicised settler mother.

Warner has the family resemblance right. Federici gives it a mechanism. Hewitt's summary of women on money — images "carefully chosen" that "reflect and reinforce our conflicting perceptions of women" \[^12\] — under this reading gets a stronger verb. The coin does not reflect a conflict; it resolves it, in capital's favour, by letting the abstracted female stand for everything the concrete female has been stripped of.

\#\# XI. Caveats and pressure points

\- \*\*Periodisation.\*\* Marianne, Britannia, Justitia's modern blindfolded form, and Columbia all consolidate after Federici's core 15th–17th-century window. The argument runs through a transmission claim — that the gendered subject-form forged in primitive accumulation becomes the raw material later state iconography draws on. Plausible (Doy makes it for revolutionary France) but a claim, not a given.  
\- \*\*Difference within the template.\*\* Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, and Columbia are not the same figure. Marianne is republican and exposes the body; Britannia is imperial and armours it; Justitia sits inside the courtroom; Columbia is settler-colonial and racialised. The framework has to make those differences carry analytic weight, not flatten them.  
\- \*\*Federici's direct texts.\*\* Much of the Federici material cited here is reception literature — Mengist on \*Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women\* \[^4\]\[^28\], secondary articulations of \*Caliban and the Witch\* \[^1\]. A published version of this essay would want direct engagement with the primary texts.  
\- \*\*The missing scholarship.\*\* No paper found stages the Warner–Federici comparison head-on, or applies Federici to Marianne / Britannia / Justitia / Columbia specifically. The essay is a synthesis of two adjacent literatures, not a review of an existing one — which is both its opportunity and its exposure.

\# The Coin and the Witch: A Federician Iconography of Female Allegory

\#\# Introduction

Walk into almost any European capital and the state will greet you as a woman. She stands on the pediment of the courthouse with her scales; she rides the reverse of the coin in your pocket; she leads the people over a barricade in the national museum; she holds a torch above the harbour. She is Justice, Liberty, the Republic, the Nation. She is almost never a specific woman, and — for most of the history in which she was installed above doorways and stamped onto money — the actual women beneath her feet could neither vote, own their wages, nor testify in the courts she was made to personify. Marina Warner named the puzzle in a sentence: liberty, justice, wisdom, and charity are personified in the female form precisely against, and in tension with, women's "historic" position \[^1\]. Two centuries earlier, at the end of the eighteenth, Antoine de Baecque's \*ancien-régime\*-into-Revolution France had already fixed the technique: allegory was the mode "most suited to the incarnation of political sovereignty," and it was through "the goddesses Truth, Liberty, Abundance or Justice" that men of state, artists, and citizens "acceded to abstract thought via metaphor" \[^2\]. Sovereignty needed a face; the face it took was female; the women it took the face \*from\* were, in the same period, being written out of the polity it presided over.

This essay is about that pairing, and about a mechanism for it that art history has not usually reached for.

The mechanism belongs to Silvia Federici. In \*Caliban and the Witch\* and the essays around it, Federici argues that the 15th–17th-century witch hunts, the enclosure of the commons, and the criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy were not pre-modern residue but the constitutive violence of capitalist primitive accumulation — a process in which the female body itself was enclosed, expropriated, and converted into an unwaged reservoir of reproductive labour, and in which "new enclosures point to social reproduction, in particular women's bodies and areas of power" \[^3\]. Federici's protagonists are peasant women, midwives, and healers; her scene is the courtroom, the common field, and the household. She does not, on the whole, look up at the pediment.

Warner, for her part, does not look down into the courtroom. Her archive is "public art, especially sculpture, and painting, poetry, and classical mythology" \[^1\] — the high-cultural genealogy of the female form as vessel for civic universals. Her instinct is that the pattern is ambivalent and old, threaded through the Virgin and Athena and Joan of Arc, refracted differently by each period that takes it up. Federici's instinct is that the modern gender order is not old at all — it is manufactured, in blood, at a specific historical moment, in order to serve a specific mode of production.

Read side by side, they describe two faces of the same operation. Federici's process strips concrete women of legal, economic, and bodily standing \[^3\]. Warner's process installs the abstracted Woman on the pediment, the seal, and the coin \[^1\]. The abstract civic Maiden is conscriptable for the \*res publica\* in a way an abstract civic Man is not, because in the very period Federici is describing, the female sign has been emptied — by force — of the political-economic agency that would otherwise tie it to a particular interest, a particular knowledge, a particular common right. Allegorical Woman can stand for everything because concrete women have been permitted to stand for nothing.

The essay's claim is that this is not analogy. It is mechanism. Modern state iconography of female allegory — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia, Lady Liberty, the female figures on coinage and pediments — is not a transhistorical cultural inheritance to be read symbolically \[^1\]. It is an iconographic \*output\* of primitive accumulation \[^3\]. The same early-modern process that Federici describes as the enclosure of the commons and the disciplining of women's bodies produced the gendered subject-form that eighteenth- and nineteenth-century states then engraved onto their seals and money. Warner's iconography names the pattern; Federici's political economy supplies its mechanism. Together they turn the abstract civic Maiden into the visible face of an invisible history: the post-mortem mask of the witch, the receipt for an expropriation, stamped on the instrument of a general equivalent.

Two literatures make this argument hard to formulate and worth formulating. On one side, feminist art history has produced a rich reading of allegorical Woman but has largely done so in a culturalist register — the female sign as long Western inheritance rather than as artefact of a datable rupture. On the other side, feminist political economy following Federici has produced a rich reading of the witch, the commons, and reproductive labour, but has mostly done so without pressure from what images \*do\*. The two literatures rarely cite each other. Where they do meet — Gen Doy's Marxist iconography of women from the French Revolution forward, Žana Kozomora's Federician reading of the witch in post-Yugoslav art, the "witching of art history" current in recent contemporary-art writing, Charles Zika's argument that early-modern witch \*imagery\* was constitutive of the hunts rather than decorative to them — they meet mostly around the witch herself, not around the civic Maiden who succeeds her. The cleanly Warnerian object — Marianne on the seal, Britannia on the halfpenny, Justitia over the courthouse, Columbia on the medal — has not, so far as this essay could find, received a sustained Federician treatment.

That gap is the essay's opening. What follows is an attempt to close it, in four figures. The argument moves in three registers at once: theoretical (Warner and Federici staged against each other; the structural pairing of masculine general equivalent and feminine secondary sign that Goux and Cady derive from Aristotle); historical (four datable icons — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia — each of which consolidates in the wake of a specific accumulation and takes on the specific disciplined femininity that accumulation required); and iconographic (what happens when the abstract civic Maiden is read not as a transhistorical sign inflected by a period, but as the period's own image of the subject it has just finished producing). The four figures, so read, stop looking like distinct icons. They start looking like variations on a template — one whose grammar Warner spelled out and whose historical conditions of possibility, this essay argues, are Federici's.

A note on evidence, and on limits. No paper I could find stages the Warner–Federici comparison head-on, and none applies Federici to Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, or Columbia specifically. Much of the Federici material available in secondary literature is reception rather than the primary texts themselves. The essay is therefore a synthesis of two adjacent literatures rather than a review of an existing one — which is both its opportunity and its exposure. Its wager is that the pairing has been visible for some time on both sides of the archive, and that naming it is worth the risk of running slightly ahead of the scholarship.

The coin, in the end, is the argument's smallest and hardest exhibit. Two centuries of women on paper money, Virginia Hewitt observed, have been "carefully chosen" — figures summoned "to proclaim the wealth of a bank, the power of a state or the glory of a nation," images that "reflect and reinforce our conflicting perceptions of women and their place in society" \[^4\]. Read through Federici, that verb — \*reflect\* — is too weak. The coin does not reflect a conflict between abstract veneration and concrete dispossession. It resolves it, in capital's favour, by letting the abstracted female stand for everything the concrete female has been stripped of. What follows is an attempt to read the coin that way.

\#\# II. Warner and Federici: where they actually disagree

Warner and Federici are often invoked in the same breath by anyone gesturing at the gap between women's symbolic prominence and women's substantive standing. The gesture is not wrong, but it is loose. Warner and Federici are not two authors approaching the same problem from adjacent angles. They are two authors working on adjacent objects with different archives, different periodisations, and, ultimately, different theories of what produces what. Before the essay can put them to work in the same argument, it has to say where they actually pull apart. Six disagreements, and one live one.

\*\*The object of analysis.\*\* Warner is an iconographer and cultural historian; her unit of analysis is the image. \*Monuments and Maidens\* ranges "over the allegorical presence of the woman in the Western tradition" through "the evidence of public art, especially sculpture, and painting, poetry, and classical mythology" \[^1\] — the Virgin, Joan of Arc, Lady Liberty, Justitia, Britannia are treated as feminine forms carrying myth and meaning across centuries. Federici's unit is not the image but the labouring, reproducing body and the social relations that govern it. Her central object is the witch hunt as a mechanism through which "the enclosure of common lands through agricultural commercialization, fencing, and the creation of a beggar class" ran alongside "the accompanying enclosure of woman's bodies through the state control of their sexuality, reproduction, and political power" \[^2\]. Warner reads representations of women; Federici reads what was done to women. The two projects can look like complementary halves of one picture, but only because one author's \*figure\* is the other author's \*cost\*.

\*\*Periodisation.\*\* Warner's frame is transhistorical. Her early work traces "the iterations and innovations of the myths of the Virgin Mary and Joan of Arc as well as the feminine form of national symbols such as Lady Liberty" \[^3\] — antiquity through modernity treated as a long Western inheritance reworked across periods. Federici's frame is the opposite shape: a tight, datable rupture. The gendered order we have was \*produced\* by primitive accumulation in the 15th–17th centuries — enclosures, the criminalisation of women's autonomous knowledge, the violent reorganisation of reproduction — and the misogynist mass violence of the witch hunts was "indivisible from men's fear of women's knowledge, labor, and power" \[^2\]. Federici's readers, in turn, treat \*Caliban and the Witch\* as an argument that the elision of witch-hunting from the history of capitalism is itself an ideological operation — one that "leftist reactions" have too readily colluded with \[^4\]. For Warner, feminine allegory is a deep cultural pattern that any given period inflects. For Federici, the modern gender order is not inherited at all; it is manufactured, in blood, at a specific historical moment.

\*\*Causal direction.\*\* The two periodisations imply different causal stories. Warner's implicit direction runs from patriarchal social structure to symbolic use: women are available as vessels for civic abstraction because patriarchy has already emptied them of self-signification. Federici inverts that arrow. The gendered subject is not a cultural premise that capital then exploits; it is something capital \*makes\*, by force, in order to extract unwaged reproductive labour. Federici's evidence, restated by her reviewers, is that the socioeconomic conditions that fomented witch-hunting are the conditions of "the rise of capitalism" itself — the misogynist misinformation campaign that justified "the systematic dispossession, torture, and extermination of countless women" is the \*mechanism\* by which a new sexual division of labour was installed, not a superstructural reflection of one already in place \[^2\]. Sokolofsky, reading Federici through Marx and Foucault, sharpens the point: primitive accumulation is best understood as "the dialectical double helix of violence and compulsion" through which capital reconstitutes the body itself \[^5\]. Warner does not have, and does not need, a theory of the mode of production. Federici's whole argument is that any account of the female sign that does not have one has misdiagnosed the case.

\*\*Whose women.\*\* Warner's archive is overwhelmingly high-cultural: monumental sculpture, state iconography, canonical poetry, court painting. Federici's protagonists are peasant women — midwives, healers, beggars, common-rights holders — the "mostly peasant women" who, as Federici reminds her readers, were the actual targets of the mass mid-modern accusation \[^4\]. The disagreement is a class disagreement, and Federici would press it: a politics of imagery that reads Marianne or Britannia without reading enclosure has misidentified where the action is. Conversely, Federici's own work has comparatively little to say about how \*ruling-class\* iconography legitimised the process she is describing. Warner's terrain is largely outside Federici's frame; Federici's protagonists are largely outside Warner's.

\*\*Ambivalence versus defeat.\*\* Warner is famously ambivalent about her material. Her cultural history of the Virgin retells the myth "critically and from a feminist perspective that did not, however, deny the emotional and stealthy power of this myth, 'as spirited and as often imperceptible as an underground stream,' on women as well as men" \[^6\]. The allegorical female, in Warner, is simultaneously elevating and disempowering; a single political verdict on figures like the Virgin, or Joan of Arc, is precisely what she resists. Federici's witch hunt is not ambivalent. It is a defeat — a femicidal, dispossessive campaign whose function is to break women as a political force, "the systematic dispossession, torture, and extermination of countless women who resisted the rise of capitalism" \[^2\]. Where Warner finds polysemy, Federici finds class war.

\*\*What politics follows.\*\* Warner's politics, to the extent the work prescribes one, is demystificatory: stop mistaking the abstract Maiden for substantive female power. Federici's politics is reconstructive and material — reclaim the commons, revalue reproductive labour, and organise around the body and the means of subsistence. Her recent essays foreground "the reproductive commons women are constructing in response to the displacements caused by the new expansion of capitalist relations" \[^7\]. Notably, Federici \*does\* engage in image-politics of a kind, most conspicuously in the rehabilitation of the witch herself as an emblem of dispossessed feminine knowledge. But the icon Federici elevates is exactly the figure Warner's method would warn against treating as redemptive without remainder.

\*\*The live disagreement.\*\* These are six disagreements; the load-bearing one is a seventh, implicit in the others. Warner's framework can read Marianne, Britannia, Justitia as \*ambivalent cultural objects\* whose feminist meaning is unsettled. Federici's framework would treat that ambivalence as an ideological \*symptom\* — the residue of having abstracted away the material process (enclosure, the witch hunt, unwaged reproduction) that made the female form available for civic abstraction in the first place. Warner, conversely, would likely find Federici's account under-attentive to the agency of imagery and myth — to how symbols \*do\* things, "as spirited and as often imperceptible as an underground stream" \[^6\], rather than merely reflecting an economic substrate. One reads the coin; the other reads what had to happen to bodies before a coin could carry that face.

The essay's move is neither to arbitrate this disagreement nor to soften it. It is to hold both authors to their strongest positions and let the pressure between them do analytic work. Federici's mechanism is what tells us \*why\* the female form was available for the operation Warner describes. Warner's iconography is what tells us \*where\* and \*how\* that availability was cashed in. Neither alone accounts for the coin, the pediment, or the seal. The next section takes up the structural argument — Goux and Cady on money as masculine general equivalent, allegory as feminine matter — that lets the two frames be joined without collapsing into one another.

\# The Coin and the Witch: A Federician Iconography of Female Allegory

\#\# III. The structural argument: money as masculine equivalent, allegory as feminine matter

Warner and Federici disagree, and the disagreement is real. What makes them jointly usable — rather than simply alternative — is a piece of structural theory that neither wrote but that both need. The theory belongs to Jean-Joseph Goux, arrives in feminist scholarship largely through Diane Cady's \*The Gender of Money\*, and does something the two authors cannot do separately: it names the specific \*form\* of the pairing between the female allegorical sign and the monetary instrument on which she so often appears. It explains why the pairing is not incidental — why the coin is \*precisely\* the surface on which the disciplined female form gets stamped.

\*\*General equivalents are gendered.\*\* Goux's move, glossed by Cady, is to generalise Marx's concept of the general equivalent beyond money. In any domain — economic, linguistic, psychoanalytic — one term is placed "in a position of privilege" and all other terms "are measured in relation to it"; call the privileged term the general equivalent, and the terms it measures "secondary signs" \[^1\]. Money is the general equivalent of commodities. The Father, on Goux's reading, is the general equivalent of subjects; the phallus, of objects; the spoken word, of expression \[^1\]. What Goux insists on, and what Cady presses, is that the selection of the general equivalent is \*always gendered\*, "although how this is so may be less obvious in the case of money and language than it is in the case of the phallus or the Father" \[^1\]. The general equivalent occupies a specific set of positions — active, stable, idealised, "the measurer of all things" — and it does so \*at the expense of\*, and in structural pairing with, the passive, unstable, measurable position of the secondary sign \[^1\].

The gendering runs deep. Cady traces Goux's derivation back to Aristotle: in \*On the Generation of Animals\* and the \*Physics\*, men "play the active role in procreation, providing the form," while women are "passive receptacles who provide the chaotic matter of generation, which lacks form without the imprint of a man" \[^1\]. That is not incidental. In economics as in Aristotelian biology, "man serves as the model — the measurement — whereas women are the secondary signs that are measured and subsumed by this hierarchy" \[^1\]. The upshot Cady states plainly: "we might think of general equivalents as masculine and secondary signs as feminine" \[^1\].

\*\*This gives the coin an argument, not just a description.\*\* Once general equivalents are gendered, the coin is no longer a neutral object that happens to carry a female face. It is a \*pairing\* — the masculine measure on one side of the exchange, the feminine matter to be measured on the other. Engraving the allegorical female onto the note is not a contradiction of the coin's economic function; it is that function made visible. The note carries the active, measuring authority — the general equivalent — and the figure on it carries the passive, idealised matter to be measured. What looks like a tribute to Woman is, in the structural argument, a diagram of a hierarchy.

This is where Federici re-enters. Federici describes the same active/passive split as the lived outcome of primitive accumulation: men's labour rendered visible as waged value, women's labour rendered invisible as nature and reproduction, the female body enclosed as the site of an unwaged reservoir \[^2\]. Goux gives us the semiotic form of the split; Federici gives us its historical production. The two are the same operation seen from opposite ends. On the semiotic side, the female is fixed as the secondary sign — passive, measurable, exchangeable — and imprinted by the masculine measure. On the historical side, the female is fixed, by force, as the reproductive body — unwaged, dispossessed, criminalised when autonomous. That the same gendered subject-form appears in both registers is not a coincidence to be explained away. It is what one would expect if primitive accumulation and the semiotic hierarchy Cady names are, as this essay claims, two dimensions of one process.

\*\*Symbolic reproduction rides the same rail.\*\* Nicky Marsh's essay on reproduction and money argues that the pattern extends beyond the coin's surface into the deep structure of monetary symbolism itself. Reading Derrida on money against Mauss and Marx, Marsh shows that "the exchange of women is central to the anthropological tradition of Mauss and Claude Lévi-Strauss in a way that it is not of Marx and Simmel and yet the former's anxiety about the relationship between female reproduction and paternity is also evident in the latter" \[^3\]. Money's cultural symbolism, in her reading, performs a specific work on female sexual reproduction — "the language of maternity" and "the policing of its limits that the virgin and whore dyad enact" \[^3\]. "Both Marx's language of the commodity and Mauss' language of the gift can be read through the assumption of the exchange of female sexuality" \[^3\]. The virgin/whore dyad is not decorative; it is the internal grammar by which the monetary sign disciplines the femaleness it also carries.

Marsh's contribution matters here because it closes a loop the Goux/Cady argument leaves open. Goux and Cady give us the \*form\* of the pairing (masculine measure, feminine matter). Marsh shows the pairing at work on \*specific content\* — sexual reproduction, maternity, virginity, whoring — that Federici's history is \*about\*. The witch hunt Federici describes is, in Marsh's terms, the moment at which the "policing of maternity's limits" gets institutionalised through the criminal law; the figure on the coin, in the same terms, is the after-image of that policing carried into iconography. The coin performs, in symbolic form, work that the courtroom performed in material form. Same operation, different surfaces.

\*\*The historical evidence is blunt.\*\* Virginia Hewitt's survey of two centuries of women on paper money makes the point without theoretical scaffolding at all. "From cool allegory to haughty beauty, images of women have appeared on paper money across the world for almost 200 years"; the images are "carefully chosen to convey messages appropriate to a particular time and place"; "proud Britannias and crowned monarchs, glamour girls and cotton-pickers have all been summoned to proclaim the wealth of a bank, the power of a state or the glory of a nation"; and — the load-bearing sentence — "the ways in which female figures on mass-produced objects both reflect and reinforce our conflicting perceptions of women and their place in society" is what her book sets out to trace \[^4\]. The verb \*reflect\* is the concession; the verb \*reinforce\* is the argument. Once Goux/Cady and Federici are in the frame, \*reflect and reinforce\* becomes too weak. The coin does not merely mirror a conflict between abstract veneration and concrete dispossession. It resolves that conflict, in the sovereign's favour, by holding the two together on one metallic surface.

\*\*A present-tense test.\*\* The structural argument would be less compelling if it could not be seen operating in real currency policy. Yulianti and colleagues' semiotic analysis of Indonesia's 2022 banknote series makes precisely this move in the present tense. Banknotes carry not just economic value but "a symbolic value through an expression of cultural identity that forms a nation," and "the customary design of banknotes in most countries issued by financial authorities is the portrait of notable male leaders" \[^5\]. Bank Indonesia's 2022 issue is unusually rich in female imagery, and the semiotic analysis finds that "on the surface, Indonesian banknotes in the 2022 emission year seem to show a sense of appreciation for women and femininity in Indonesia but in essence still lack a sense of justice towards women" \[^5\]. That gap — appreciation on the surface, injustice underneath — is the Goux/Cady pairing operating in the twenty-first century. The general equivalent takes a female face; the substantive standing the face signifies is withheld. Two hundred years after Marianne, the operation runs on the same logic.

\*\*What this gets us before the case studies.\*\* The essay has now three moving parts. Warner supplies the iconographic pattern: the female form as vessel for civic universals. Federici supplies the mechanism: primitive accumulation as the violent production of a gendered subject fitted to serve that use. Goux, through Cady, supplies the structural pairing that lets the mechanism ride the icon: the general equivalent is masculine, the secondary sign feminine, and the coin is the surface on which the two are locked together. Marsh supplies the specific content — sexual reproduction, maternity, the virgin/whore dyad — on which the pairing works. Hewitt supplies the two-century empirical record of the coin behaving as the argument predicts. And the Indonesian case supplies a present-tense demonstration that the pattern still runs on its Aristotelian rails.

What the essay has not yet done is show the operation on specific icons — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia. Each of those figures is a case study in the same structure realised under different accumulations: republican-insurrectionary in France, imperial-conservative in Britain, judicial-institutional in the state form itself, settler-colonial in America. The next four sections take them in turn.

\#\# IV. Reading Marianne — the citizen-woman who is not a citizen

Marianne is the natural first case because the pairing Section III describes runs so close to the surface of her early history that it is difficult to miss once one is looking. The Republic that engraves her on its seal in 1792 does so in the same window in which it forecloses the political existence of actual women; the icon of civic universality and the exclusion of women from civic universality are produced by the same body, in the same year, and in explicit knowledge of each other. Marianne is not a symbol whose feminism has faded; she is a symbol whose feminism was never available, because the polity that made her had just refused it.

\*\*The exclusion that makes the sign available.\*\* Olympe de Gouges publishes her \*Déclaration des droits de la femme et de la citoyenne\* in 1791, addressed directly to the National Assembly, arguing that "no society has a constitution without the guarantee of rights and the separation of powers" and that women must be "constituted into a National Assembly" so that the "authoritative acts of women and the authoritative acts of men" are equally privileged \[^1\]. She is explicit about what she is doing: she likens "the civil status of French women maritally contracted into servitude to that of Africans sold into colonial slavery," and negates both institutions as then construed \[^1\]. Her opening question — "Man, are you capable of being just?" — is answered, two years later, by the guillotine \[^1\]. The polity that executed Gouges in 1793 is the polity that had put Marianne on its seal in 1792\. It is the same polity because it is the same operation: the \*concrete\* female citizen is removed, and the \*abstract\* female citizen is installed in her place.

That the substitution runs at the level of visual culture and not just law is Vovelle's finding: over the course of the Revolution, popular imagery moves "from feminine to masculine symbolism" as the political-female recedes and the polity's own iconography reorganises around male types \[^2\]. Marianne survives that movement, but she survives it as \*residue\* — the one female form the Republic keeps, purified of the political-female claims the same Republic has just been silencing. Warner's framework can describe this as ambivalence. Federici's, and the structural argument of Section III, describe it as the operation working as designed: the general equivalent is masculine, the secondary sign feminine, and the female sign becomes available for civic universalisation exactly to the degree that concrete women have been evacuated from civic standing.

\*\*Cleansed of every dangerous attribute.\*\* The specific iconographic content Marianne accumulates is, on inspection, a negative image of the peasant woman Federici's witch hunt describes. Cherneski's reading of the Marianne of 1792 catches this precisely: she "represented the virtues so desired by the new order: the transcendence of localism, superstition, and particularity in the name of a more disciplined and universalistic worship" — she "belonged to no group, to no particular place" and was "the antithesis of those 'ridiculous usages, gothic formulas, absurd and puerile etiquette, and the right usurped by the clergy,' which radicals had already denounced in 1790" \[^3\]. Read that list of virtues against Federici's account of who was burned. The witch trials of the 15th–17th centuries targeted women whose knowledge and authority were \*local\* — the midwife, the healer, the common-rights holder — women whose practice was \*particular\*, whose lore might well be called \*superstitious\* by the emerging natural philosophy, whose economic base was the common land that enclosure was privatising \[^4\]. Marianne is what remains once those attributes have been extinguished from concrete women and, in extinguished form, are then affirmatively \*inverted\* into republican virtues. She is placeless because her historical counterpart was driven off her place; she is universal because the particular was criminalised in the peasant woman; she is disciplined because the peasant woman had been disciplined first. The icon is the mould from which the historical figure has been struck out.

\*\*The politicised breast.\*\* The bared breast of Delacroix's \*Liberty Leading the People\*, and the wider iconography of the exposed republican female that Cha's study traces from the ancient into the modern — "le sein des femmes est reconnu comme un symbole de maternité et de sexualité" \[^5\] — is where the argument gets sharpest. Warner's reading of the maternal breast in civic allegory treats it as ambivalent, at once nurturing and objectifying, feminist and patriarchal. A Federician reading refuses the ambivalence at this specific historical joint. The bourgeois Republic is staging, as its own official insignia, exactly the bodily surface — nurturing, reproductive, visible, unshamed — for which peasant women had been persecuted when they claimed it as autonomous. The autonomous breast in the village (the wet-nurse's, the healer's, the midwife's) is dangerous, punishable, sometimes lethal to its owner; the same breast, rendered abstract and marble-clean on the Republic's frontispiece, is patriotic. The transposition is not a paradox to be resolved through symbolic analysis. It is the operation itself: the female bodily attribute is expropriated from the concrete woman, cleansed of her, and re-mounted on the icon of the polity that expropriated it. The politicised breast on the Republic's flag is the same breast the state has just criminalised in the village — held up as the state's own.

\*\*The material other side.\*\* The Revolution's land reforms, in the same period, privatise commons whose loss falls most heavily on women. Joan Humphries's study of English enclosure names a mechanism that has direct French analogues: "women and children were the primary exploiters of common rights, \[and\] their loss led to changes in women's economic position within the family and more generally to increased dependence of whole families on wages and wage earners" \[^6\]. The general logic is not specific to England. Where common rights are extinguished and land is privatised, the female subsistence base is disproportionately hit; the woman is driven either into the wage form (mediated through the male earner she now depends on) or into the household as unwaged reproducer. Marianne's iconography of "liberty" — the free citizen, unattached to particular place — circulates in exactly this economic register. The freedom in question is the freedom-to-be-proletarianised. It hits women's economic standing first and hardest. The insurrectionary maiden on the barricade and the woman driven off the common are the same historical figure, seen from opposite sides: the icon is what the ideology exports, the enclosed woman is what the political economy imports.

\*\*The pairing on the coin.\*\* The final move is to put Marianne back inside the structural argument of Section III. Antoine de Baecque's account of French allegory in the late eighteenth century is precise about the medium: allegory becomes "the most suited to the incarnation of political sovereignty," a "visual narrative" that "told the story of political power through metaphor," carrying "the emblems of revolutionary power" and "embodying this power through the incarnation of revolutionary values" \[^7\]. What is at stake is nothing less than "how to find a metaphorical language that could express, using figurative images — for example the goddesses Truth, Liberty, Abundance or Justice — the supreme values of a regime, a government, a State" \[^7\]. It is through this allegorical repertoire that "men of state, artists, and citizens acceded to abstract thought via metaphor" \[^7\]. Baecque's language repays close reading. The subject who \*accedes\* is coded male ("men of state, artists, and citizens" — in a polity that had just refused female citizenship). The medium of that ascent is female. Marianne is not the one who thinks; she is the figure by which the male citizen is enabled to think abstractly. She occupies the passive matter position of Goux/Cady's schema exactly: the general equivalent that measures the polity's ideals is masculine (the sovereign, the citizen, the abstract measure); the secondary sign on which those ideals are stamped is feminine (Marianne, and after her Truth, Liberty, Abundance, Justice) \[^8\]. The coin is where this pairing is materialised in daily circulation. Marianne on the franc is the passive matter stamped by the active masculine measure — she is what the coin \*shows\* while the coin \*does\* something else.

The Napoleonic Code of 1804 closes the loop. The Republic that put Marianne on its seal in 1792 and executed Gouges in 1793 \[^1\] formalises by 1804 that women are civilly subordinate — that the wife owes obedience, that she cannot conduct legal acts without her husband's authorisation, that she is not, in the operative sense, a citizen. Marianne's face is on the state's currency and its seal; the flesh-and-blood woman is legally outside it. This is not a failure of the icon to keep its promise. It is the icon delivering exactly what it was engineered to deliver: the veneration of the abstract Female on the surface of an instrument whose material effect is the disciplining of concrete women. Once one reads Marianne this way, the ambivalence Warner's framework identifies stops looking like a puzzle in the image and starts looking like the image working correctly. The residue of the extinguished peasant woman, the abstracted mother-breast of the polity, the passive matter under the sovereign's stamp, the citizen-woman who is not a citizen — these are not four readings of one figure. They are one operation seen four times.

Britannia, in the next section, will run the same argument through a different accumulation: not enclosure and republican exclusion, but empire and settler colonialism. What Marianne shows in the register of \*revolutionary founding\*, Britannia will show in the register of \*imperial maturity\* — the disciplined female form as the acceptable face of an accumulation that runs on other continents.

\#\# V. Reading Britannia — the conquered woman who becomes the conqueror's brand

If Marianne shows the operation of primitive accumulation in the register of \*revolutionary founding\* — enclosure and the exclusion of women from a new citizenship — Britannia shows it in the register of \*imperial maturity\*. She is the disciplined female form on the surface of a coin whose material function is the general equivalent of an empire: the currency of enclosure at home and dispossession abroad, of the Atlantic slave system and the East India Company. Her iconographic register is the opposite of Marianne's. Marianne is bare-breasted, insurrectionary, exposed on the barricade; Britannia is helmeted, seated, armoured, calm. But the difference is variation on a single template. What Marianne shows as republican residue Britannia shows as imperial matron. Both are what remains once concrete women have been evacuated from the historical scene the icon presides over.

\*\*Origin as conquest image.\*\* The genealogy is embarrassing, and the iconographic scholarship notes it without embarrassment. Cusack's study is direct: "the early images of Britannia appeared on the reverse of coins minted to celebrate the victories of the second century Roman Emperor Hadrian: Hadrian's portrait is on one side and Britannia's profile on the reverse represented the conquered island" \[^1\]. The female form the British state later adopts to stamp its money was, in her first life, the personification of an \*expropriated\* territory. She is a Roman coin's advertisement of somebody else's primitive accumulation. When the British state revives her in the seventeenth century, models her on Athena — "Britannia was modelled on Athena, the Greek goddess of warfare and wisdom" — and puts her on Charles II's halfpenny, she brings that provenance with her \[^1\]. By the 1821 coinage she "wears Athena's helmet and she retained military garb over the nineteenth century"; her trident is Neptune's, "in acknowledgement of Britain's naval power" \[^1\]. The genealogy is not decorative. Britannia is, on her own iconographic terms, a coin-image of empire — first of an empire that conquered Britain, then, through a two-thousand-year inversion, of an empire that conquered other continents from Britain. The female sign migrates, but the semiotic slot she occupies — the personified territory whose subjection the coin celebrates — does not.

\*\*Enclosure and empire on one coin.\*\* The temporal coincidence is worth naming. Britannia's mass appearance on British coinage is not a Restoration curiosity; it runs the length of the accumulation. Charles II's halfpenny of 1672 opens the run; the great recoinage of 1797, under Pitt, consolidates it; her presence on the penny, the halfpenny, and the two-pound coin extends into the twentieth century. That period is the \*long\* parliamentary enclosure — 1750 to 1830 — the East India Company's rise and consolidation, the peak and abolition of the Atlantic slave trade, and the industrialisation of the north of England on cotton produced by enslaved labour in the Americas. The coin that circulates through those transactions is the coin that bears Britannia. Section III's structural argument makes the pairing legible: the coin is the general equivalent, the female form is the secondary sign, and what the pairing does is materialise, on a single object exchanged millions of times a day, the hierarchy that primitive accumulation instituted \[^2\]. Federici's account of that accumulation — the enclosure of the commons, the criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy, the conversion of the female body into an unwaged reservoir \[^3\] — is the process of which Britannia is the receipt. Every halfpenny is a small monument to the fact that the accumulation happened and that its ideological cover has a face.

\*\*The manufactured femininity, enthroned.\*\* The most striking piece of the case is what Britannia is \*like\*. Cusack's description repays close reading: "Britannia's body language typically conveys self-possession, self-discipline, and superior calm: she is rarely depicted as agitated or over-indulging" \[^1\]. She "embodied the Victorian upper-class value of moderation, which extended to various aspects of genteel societal presentation for both women and men" \[^1\]. That list — self-possession, self-discipline, superior calm, moderation, absence of agitation, absence of over-indulgence — reads as a Victorian gentility profile. It also reads, almost line for line, as the femininity Federici argues the witch hunt manufactured. Kozomora restates Federici's argument in exactly this idiom: the witch trials enforced "a new model of femininity for a capitalist society: sexless, obedient, submissive, and subordinate" \[^4\]. Britannia is that manufactured femininity \*enthroned\*. Where Marianne stages the exposed republican breast, Britannia armours the classical torso; where Marianne is placeless and insurrectionary, Britannia is seated, moderate, and permanent. The two icons express different accumulations under different regimes, but the femininity each idealises is the femininity Federici's process produced — Marianne cleansed of localism, Britannia cleansed of agitation. The peasant woman of Federici's archive is agitated: she resists enclosure, she withholds reproductive labour, she practices lore the new order cannot license, she is dragged into court and burned. Britannia is the icon that has been engineered so that she cannot be that woman. She is the composite negative: everything the witch was, Britannia refuses to be. Her calm is not a character trait. It is the residue of the burning.

The class specificity Cusack notes matters here. Neoclassicism was fashionable among the "British upper classes from the second half of the eighteenth century"; "young men from affluent families, although generally not women, received a classical education, and the ruling elites associated such figures with 'prestige and power \[and\] moral values'"; "Britannia's provenance as ancient and classical matched the aspirations of the Victorian upper classes, as well as the newly powerful middle classes" \[^1\]. The pairing this recovers is precise. The classical education that gives men the interpretive competence to read Britannia is denied to women; the figure they read is female. The icon is the male citizen's Athena. Britannia is the object of a gaze that has been trained on the classical female form by an education system from which the actual female is excluded — a small local instance of the general Goux/Cady pairing: masculine measure, feminine matter, active reader, passive icon \[^2\].

\*\*The colonial inflection.\*\* Britannia is also \*Mother Country\*, and this is where the reading becomes properly imperial. Alessio's study of the female personifications of the white settler colonies — Australia, Canada, New Zealand, South Africa — is precise about the propaganda function: British imperial propaganda has "generally been perceived as a male arena," but "women were central to empire imagery, particularly when they were used as symbols of the white colonies," and such female personifications "became ideal images for attracting both male and female emigrants" and "reflects the growing national prominence of women as they evolved into imperial agents for preventing racial and moral decline" \[^5\]. The maternal-allegorical idiom does specific work. Britannia is the elder female; her daughter-colonies are the younger. The family group — Britannia in the centre, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and South Africa around her — appears repeatedly in late-Victorian and Edwardian imperial imagery. What the imagery does is transpose the settler-colonial relation into a domestic-reproductive relation: the mother country reproduces herself, biologically and morally, through her daughters. The women hailed by that imagery — as emigrants, as wives, as reproducers of the settler population — become, in Alessio's phrasing, "imperial agents." The icon is a recruitment poster for reproductive labour.

The other side of that recruitment is the reproductive labour of colonised women. Bhattacharya's study of "Breast, Womb, Empire" names the specific colonial category the domestic register requires: the \*amah\*, the \*dhaye\*, the \*ayah\* — women "living in colonial India and hired by the Empire, particularly the British memsahibs," who "become foster-mothers to the future progeny of the Empire" \[^6\]. The relation is stated with unusual sharpness: "for the colonized amah/ayah it is the brown breast/body that binds the oppressor with the oppressed, uniting them and making them one" \[^6\]. The category is old — "the notion of hiring racially othered laborers from a certain socioeconomic class under the guise of economic development and 'civilizing' expansion is a concept as old as colonization itself" \[^6\]. Britannia, the classical maternal allegory on the coin, is the visible face of an invisible reproductive division of labour: the settler mother whose empire runs on the outsourced breast and womb of colonised women. Marianne's breast, in Section IV, was the peasant woman's breast abstracted onto the polity. Britannia's breast is the same breast at a different scale of accumulation: the \*amah's\* breast rendered classical, whitened, armoured, and put on the coin that pays her.

\*\*A note on register.\*\* Britannia and Marianne are not the same figure, and the argument has to make that difference carry weight. Marianne is republican, partly insurrectionary, exposes the body, and consolidates in the founding moment of a polity that has just guillotined its most articulate female critic. Britannia is imperial, conservative, armours the body, and consolidates over the long century of parliamentary enclosure and imperial expansion. The register is different because the accumulation is different: Marianne is the icon of enclosure-plus-republican-exclusion at home; Britannia is the icon of enclosure-plus-empire, at home \*and\* abroad. What is the same is the semiotic slot the female form occupies — the general equivalent's passive matter — and the mechanism by which the concrete female has been evacuated to make the slot available. Federici's account of primitive accumulation has always been plural. It runs through enclosure, through the witch hunt, through the criminalisation of women's autonomous labour, through the slave trade, through colonial expropriation \[^3\]\[^4\]. Britannia is the figure on which the plural is stamped. She is, in a phrase, primitive accumulation with a helmet.

Section VI turns to Justitia, who lets the argument step \*inside\* the state — from the coin the state issues to the courtroom the state runs, and to the female sign that has presided over that courtroom throughout the witch trials themselves.

\# The Coin and the Witch — Section VI: Justitia

\# The Coin and the Witch: A Federician Iconography of Female Allegory

\#\# VI. Reading Justitia — the blindfolded judge whose court tried the witches

The three preceding case studies keep the argument on the surface of the coin. Justitia moves it inside the building. She is the female allegory that presides not over the currency of the state but over its most consequential organ — the courtroom that heard the witch trials, sentenced the accused, and set the legal precedents through which the criminalisation of women's autonomous knowledge and reproductive labour was actually done. If Federici's argument is that the witch hunt was constitutive of primitive accumulation, then the icon on the pediment of the court that ran the witch hunt is not a decoration to be read symbolically. She is the institutional signature. Reading her demands a slightly different move than reading Marianne or Britannia: not the substitution of the abstract female for a concrete female evacuated from citizenship or from the coin's economy, but the \*inversion\* of a specific attribute — blindness — from a critique of arbitrary justice into an emblem of impartial one, in exactly the window in which the courts became least impartial toward women.

\*\*A datable icon inside a long genealogy.\*\* Justitia is medieval and Roman in origin — she predates Federici's window by a millennium. That fact is important, and the argument has to be careful not to over-claim. The specifically \*modern\* Justitia — the female form with scales, sword, and blindfold, standing or seated on the courthouse pediment or seal — is a consolidation that happens on a datable timeline, and the timeline is the timeline of the witch hunt.

The blindfold is the decisive feature, because its history is precise enough to argue with. Hertogh's careful reconstruction puts the origin: "At the end of the fifteenth century, Lady Justice's blindfold was used as a negative attribute. The earliest known representation of a blindfolded Lady Justice is a satirical woodcut for Sebastian Brant's \*Das Narrenschiff\* (\*The Ship of Fools\*, 1494), in which the author criticised the abuse of trials and the foolishness of court quarellings" \[^1\]. Brant's blindfolded Justitia is a \*bad\* Justitia. She cannot see, and her blindness is proof that the courts have gone wrong: "the abuse of trials and the foolishness of court quarellings" is what her blindfold indexes. The image is a critique.

Over the following two centuries the blindfold reverses valence. Cardoso and Miluzzi describe it as one of the standing "conceptual trompe l'oeuil" of the iconographic imagery of Justice — "whether Justice is shown as blindfolded or not," a marker that at different times meant "either as a critique of arbitrary justice or as a symbol of Justice's impartiality" \[^2\]. Their reading of what the switch does to interpretation is sharp: the widespread representation of Justice in figurative arts is "ultimately flawed by political overtones and distant from the truly essence of the jurisdictional phenomenon"; "a few exceptions of artworks and famous literary trials come much closer to grasping the essence of Justice through due process, and are way more instructive than its deified and standardized allegory" \[^2\]. The complaint is aimed at the icon as such — the standardised allegory is misleading — and it names, from the inside of legal scholarship, exactly the pattern this essay is trying to describe.

Prosperi's \*Justice Blindfolded\* provides the century-by-century tracking, and its window is Section VI's window. The book is subtitled "\*The Historical Course of an Image\*" and set in the "Catholic Christendom, 1300–1700" series \[^3\]. That the definitive iconographic history of the modern blindfolded Justitia is bounded on the closing side by 1700 is not a coincidence to be argued around. The window in which the blindfold flips from a critique of arbitrary justice into an emblem of impartial justice is the window in which the European witch trials rise, peak, and taper. The window of the icon \*is\* the window of the hunt.

\*\*The courtroom made the witch.\*\* The Federician move at this juncture is to refuse to treat the courtroom as a neutral instrument that occasionally malfunctioned. Karlsen's \*The Devil in the Shape of a Woman\*, on witchcraft in colonial New England, states the case with a precision that has aged well. The accused — Mary Johnson, executed in Connecticut in 1648; Ann Hibbens, the wealthy Boston widow, hanged in 1656; the Hartford outbreak of 1662, a generation before Salem — are the study's opening list of proper names, and the study's question is why \*these women\*, why \*now\*: "More than three hundred years later the question still haunts us: Why were these and other women likely witches? Why were they vulnerable to accusations of witchcraft?" \[^4\]. The answer Karlsen supplies is a structural one: her work "reveals the social construction of witchcraft in seventeenth-century New England and illuminates the larger contours of gender relations in that society" \[^4\]. Witchcraft is a construction; the courtroom is the site of its construction; the woman in the dock is disproportionately the woman whose property or autonomy exceeds the patriarchal norm. The court did not adjudicate a fact about witches. It produced the category, and produced it on gendered bodies.

Federici's argument stated at the general level says the same thing at continental scale. As Mengist restates it: witch-hunting was "indivisible from men's fear of women's knowledge, labor, and power," and it drove "the systematic dispossession, torture, and extermination of countless women who resisted the rise of capitalism" \[^5\]. The instrument of that dispossession was the court. Every conviction was a judicial act — a warrant issued, a magistrate presiding, a scribe recording. Whatever iconography that court happened to be sitting under mattered less than the machinery running behind it. But if one asks what iconography the machinery in fact sat under, the answer is the iconography whose modern form was consolidating in exactly that century. Justitia's blindfold was flipping from vice to virtue at the same time the courtrooms she watched over were producing witches.

\*\*The Federician reading.\*\* Put those two things together and the icon acquires a different function. The blindfold as \*impartiality\* is a claim: the female judge does not see the sex, the class, the property status, or the parish of the accused; she weighs the evidence indifferently. The blindfold as \*ideological cover\* is a description: the courtroom in fact sees, all too well, whose knowledge is threatening and whose property is out of line, but the icon on the pediment testifies that it does not. What Warner's framework would read as polysemy — sighted or blindfolded, throned or humble, armed or unarmed, at different moments meaning different things \[^2\] — a Federician reading treats as the standing solution to a specific problem. The problem is that the court must produce, over and over, a systematically gendered outcome (the accused disproportionately female, disproportionately propertied outside male lines, disproportionately autonomous in knowledge and labour) while claiming to produce a neutral one. The icon is the standing answer. The female sign of impartial justice functions precisely by \*not seeing\* the sex/class/race asymmetry of who is in the dock. It is not that Justitia's blindfold conceals the identity of a particular defendant. It conceals the pattern of defendants.

The gender of the icon does specific work here — work that Marianne and Britannia both do in their own registers but that Justitia sharpens. If the judge on the seal were male, his blindness would raise the wrong question: \*why is he not looking?\* If the judge is female, her blindness sits inside a preexisting cultural expectation that the female sign is the passive matter, not the active measurer \[^6\]. Section III's structural pairing is doing the ideological labour on Justitia's pediment. She is the vessel; the sovereign is the measurer; the judgment is what the sovereign renders through her. Her blindness is not a personal virtue. It is the sign that she is \*not the one who sees\* — and therefore not the one to be held accountable when what the court sees is patterned by the interests of the accumulation on whose money her fellow allegory (Britannia, Marianne) is stamped.

\*\*The racial extension.\*\* The argument does not stay in the seventeenth century. Capers's "On Justitia, Race, Gender, and Blindness" transposes it, without losing precision, to the American criminal-legal register: "what does it mean, connotatively and denotatively, for Justitia to be blind in a racialized society where color is so determinative? And conversely, what does it mean to fix a black gaze upon an image of justice that has been figured as white and female?" \[^7\]. Capers's two questions are the same question the essay has been putting to the other icons, in a different register. The female sign of impartial justice functions by \*not seeing\* — first the peasant woman in the dock in 1650, then the racialised defendant in the dock in 1950 or 2020\. The institutional descent is direct. The courtroom whose iconography was consolidated during the witch trials is the courtroom that later heard slave codes, vagrancy laws, and the racialised criminal law of the American twentieth century. Federici's late work makes exactly this generalising move, extending the argument beyond early-modern Europe into the postcolonial Global South. Justitia is the icon that lets that continuity be denied at the very moment it is being enforced.

\*\*A note on periodisation.\*\* Justitia strains the essay's argument at exactly the point Section II's disagreement between Warner and Federici predicted she would. She is, in one obvious sense, transhistorical — a female personification of law present from Roman antiquity through medieval iconography, well before the witch hunt. The Federician argument therefore has to be careful about what it claims. It does not claim that Justitia was invented in the sixteenth century. It claims that her specifically \*modern\* form — the female figure whose blindfold connotes impartiality rather than corruption, whose posture consolidates as self-possessed and armoured, whose iconographic slot in the state's public architecture becomes standardised — was consolidated in the same window in which the courtroom she presides over was performing the systematic gendered violence Federici describes. The icon predates the accumulation. The icon's \*modern form\* is coextensive with it.

Section VII turns to Columbia and Lady Liberty, where the argument encounters two further complications the previous three icons did not force: a New World setting, in which primitive accumulation runs through indigenous dispossession and racialised slavery rather than parliamentary enclosure; and an explicit iconographic substitution — the Indian Princess replaced by the Greek Goddess — that stages the accumulation in the visual archive itself.

\# The Coin and the Witch — Sections VIII–IX: The bridge literature and where it's still missing

\# The Coin and the Witch: A Federician Iconography of Female Allegory

\#\# VIII. Who has tried to bridge Warner and Federici

The four case studies of Sections IV–VII are, so far as this essay could determine, synthetic — no published work stages the argument that Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, and Columbia are one template with four variations. But the essay is not proposing to build the bridge on empty ground. A scattered but real body of scholarship has been working the space between Warner's iconographic tradition and Federici's political economy, mostly in one direction: from Federici into visual studies, much less from visual studies back into political economy. The current has enough coherence to name, and enough limits to name too. This section catalogues the closest predecessors; Section IX says what they leave undone.

\*\*Gen Doy, \*Seeing and Consciousness: Women, Class and Representation\* (1995).\*\* Doy is the closest programmatic predecessor for a Marxist-feminist iconography of women, and she predates Federici's mainstream uptake. Her project is stated directly: the book "challenges the widespread belief that Marxism has nothing valuable to contribute to women's studies" through "its provocative examination of feminist and Marxist approaches to women's art and female representations" \[^1\]. The load-bearing claim: "from the French Revolution through to the present, gender \*and\* class have shaped visual imagery," and Marxist theory can be used "to question some of the premises of feminist art histories and to provide a more accurate understanding of the meaning(s) of visual imagery" \[^1\]. Her chapter list itself sketches a periodisation the present essay could almost import wholesale: "Marxist theory, feminism(s) and women's history women and the bourgeois revolution of 1789 — artists, mothers and markers of (art) history gender and class in early modernist painting — a reassessment women, class and photography — the Paris commune of 1871 Russia and the Soviet Union ca.1880–ca.1940 — 'patriarchal' culture or 'totalitarian androgeny'? women and Nazi art — Nazi women and art the postmodern, gender and race" \[^2\]. Doy's revolutionary-France chapter is, effectively, the political-economic exterior of Section IV's Marianne — the class analysis of the female sign that consolidates in 1789–1793. What Doy does not have, because \*Caliban and the Witch\* had not yet been written in her form, is Federici's specific mechanism: the witch hunt and the disciplined reproductive body as the constitutive violence of primitive accumulation. Doy has the class analysis without the reproductive-labour argument. The two arguments are compatible; nobody has combined them on the object of civic female allegory.

\*\*Žana Kozomora, "To Conjure Her" (2022).\*\* Kozomora is the most explicit single-essay bridge this essay's search located. She reads contemporary ex-Yugoslav artists — Selma Selman and Šejla Kamerić — "through the figure of the Slavic 'witch' via feminist scholar Silvia Federici" \[^3\]. What makes the essay a genuine bridge, rather than another application of Federici to contemporary art, is that Kozomora holds both frames at once. She acknowledges the periodisation problem head-on: "the South Slavic Balkans didn't experience the particular witch-hunts that Federici's reading of history charts," but Federici's theory "helps consider contemporary conditions that reflect neocolonialism, particularly the outbreak of the 1990s Yugoslav war with the official destruction of socialism and transition to fully fleshed capitalism" — and if that violent transition "casts women as witches, then the degree of power they held was reaffirmed as a threat to the construction of the new capitalist nation-state" \[^3\]. She then names the Warnerian half of the object explicitly: during the war, women were "classed as 'national traitors' by an increasingly dominant, chauvinistic discourse that understood women as metaphoric markers of the national body, a symbolic battlefield in which women's bodies…" carry the mark of the nation \[^3\]. That formulation — the woman as \*metaphoric marker of the national body, a symbolic battlefield\* — is exactly the Warnerian object (the female form as vessel for civic universals) held inside a Federician frame (primitive accumulation, capitalist transition, criminalisation of female autonomy). Kozomora does what Warner's tradition rarely does — names the political economy under the symbol — and what Federici rarely does — works through specific images and specific artists. What the essay is not is what this essay is trying to be: it operates on contemporary art rather than on the historical civic allegories that consolidated the template.

\*\*The "witching of art history" current — Deepwell, Naessens, Kokkinen, Turley-Sonne.\*\* A loose but recognisable body of work from the late 2010s onward uses Federici as theoretical scaffolding for reading the witch in contemporary art. Deepwell's 2015 ICA conference \*Misogyny: Witches and Wicked Bodies\* asked directly whether historical witch imagery is "'woman-hating' or misogynist" and what feminist artists do with it — the panel included Alexandra Kokoli, Lynne Segal, and Deepwell herself \[^4\]. Naessens's study of contemporary practice traces a broad genealogy: many contemporary artists "call upon the iconography of witchcraft, rewriting its past stories, exploring current ones, or actualizing practices and gestures (Camille Ducellier, Tatiana Karl Pez, Myriam Mihindou)"; "beyond folklore and esotericism, the witch is for these artists a figure of female emancipation with powerful subversive potential"; "since the creation of the W.I.T.C.H. and the birth of anti-nuclear movements in the United States in the 1970s, women have emerged as producers of rituals capable of generating collective power from different perspectives of struggle (feminist, ecologist, anti-capitalist)"; and this "militant projection is now resurfacing with force, particularly through the first gatherings of Witch Bloc Paris" \[^5\]. Kokkinen frames the whole project as a methodological turn: "witching" functions "as a political term, inviting a reimagining of normative approaches to art history and its intersections with esotericism, occultism and magic," and reflects "how artists have reclaimed the figure of 'the witch' — once stigmatised as evil and perverse — and transformed it into a symbol of empowerment" \[^6\]. Turley-Sonne's \*Art & Magic\* attempts a long genealogy: "Art Witches have created powerful images that resonate with beauty and activism, from Italian courtrooms in the 1500s to binding spells for the Trump presidency in 2016"; the book "for the first time… connects the genealogy of the image of the witch from historical artists to contemporary artists" and asks how the narrative is "exposed and curated in museums and memorials focused on witchcraft" \[^7\].

The limit of the "witching" current, from the point of view of the argument this essay is trying to build, is worth naming precisely. Federici is cited \*for the witch\* — for the history that authorises the rehabilitation of the figure — but not for the iconographic argument the essay wants. These writers import her political economy to license their reading of images; they do not reciprocally pressure her theory with what images do. The direction of traffic is one-way. And the object is the witch, not the civic Maiden. What the current shows is that the traffic \*between\* the two literatures is real, and has produced a decade of interesting work; what it does not show is that anyone has yet run the traffic on Warner's canonical objects.

\*\*Jaleh Mansoor, "Militant Landscape" (2021).\*\* Mansoor's is a different kind of bridge — not the witch but landscape, not iconography of the female body but of the extractive terrain. She reads Turner's \*Rain, Steam, and Speed\* (1844) — "the first landscape painting to both articulate the ontological shifts brought about by new modes of extraction and production, but also to suggest concomitant transformation in perception" — against Marx's 1844 \*Manuscripts\*, and then reads Zachary Formwalt's 2013 \*Projective Geometry\*, which reads aloud from Chapter 25 of \*Capital\* ("So-called Primitive Accumulation") over "footage — each shot organized in rigorous single point perspective — of the railroad built by England, France, and Belgium extending from the Ivory Coast to the Cape" \[^8\]. Formwalt also "unearthed documents from archives in those Imperialist nation states and former/present empires casually mentioning the now unruly, now obedient, yet always 'pesky' local African labor that lost life to the enterprise" \[^8\]. Mansoor's essay analyses both works "crossing Modernism, aka the culture wing of Modernity (itself a polite term for the ontological shifts brought about by the capitalist mode of production) through the theoretical matrix of Marx, Luxemburg, Sohn-Rethel, and Courtauld" \[^8\]. The witch is absent, and so is the female civic allegory. But the \*move\* is the one Warner's tradition would need for the present essay: iconographic analysis whose substrate is primitive accumulation, not transhistorical myth. Mansoor demonstrates that art history can be written this way. She does not do it for Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, or Columbia.

\*\*Marina Vishmidt, "Speaking of Bodies and Bodies That Speak" (2017).\*\* Vishmidt supplies the theoretical hygiene the argument needs to avoid two adjacent errors. The first is a naïve return to "the body" as a pre-political ground; the second is a culturalist reading of the body as pure signifier. Vishmidt refuses both. She notes that the post-poststructuralist turn to "'the body' as the central signifier of much poststructuralist and post-foundationalist cultural theory and philosophy" and to "'bodies' as the loci of political agency ('bodies assembling', 'Parliament of Bodies')" tends to produce "the possibility of pseudo-concreteness that often accompanies theoretical projects intolerant of (real) abstraction" \[^9\]. She names the specific ideological cost: "the discourse of the body and of bodies elides the question of how and why bodies are produced, accepting the bio-, if not necro-, political, premises of the current dispensation — one that capitalises on the fragile, isolated and suffering body — and converting them into an ethical victory" \[^9\]. This "tends to exemplify rather than challenge a scenario in which 'the reproduction of capitalism and the reproduction of organisms become indistinguishable'" \[^9\]. The question Vishmidt puts at the centre — "what form of social relation makes such a thing as 'the body' not just legible but a departure point for any political subjectivation?" \[^9\] — is the question this essay's argument has been pressing on the four icons. The female body on the coin, on the courthouse pediment, on the seal, is not a pre-political ground; it is the \*result\* of a specific social relation (primitive accumulation, in Federici's account), staged as if it were the ground.

\*\*Maria Daskalaki (2021), on Federici and self-reproducing movements.\*\* Daskalaki's contribution is more sociological than iconographic, but it is worth naming here because it does something the "witching of art history" current does not: it operationalises Federici's witch as an \*organising\* figure, not just an image to be recuperated. Her essay draws on \*Caliban and the Witch\* directly to think about "crises, struggles over social reproduction, and feminist activist organizing," and works through concrete cases — "the Parisian pétroleuses (the female supporters of the Paris Commune), the Kurdish Women's Movement in Rojava, the Urban Land committees in Venezuela, and the 21st century witch-hunting in Africa" — to argue that "the struggles over social reproduction are intertwined with resistances that enable women to participate in communities that re-embed them in the spheres of feminist activism" \[^10\]. This matters for the essay's argument in an indirect but real way: it establishes that Federici's framework, applied outside the early-modern European window, retains explanatory purchase. The extension from \*Caliban\* into the postcolonial and contemporary world is not this essay's invention. It is a live current in the reception literature. Marianne and Britannia and Columbia sit inside that longer arc.

\*\*Charles Zika, \*The Appearance of Witchcraft: Print and Visual Culture in Sixteenth-Century Europe\* (2007).\*\* The essay saves Zika for last because his contribution runs the traffic in the other direction — from art history \*into\* Federici — and does the work of materialising Warner's territory back into Federici's period. Zika's book is a "meticulous study" that "demonstrates how social and religious changes during this period came together with demonological theory, and how the resulting visual culture affected the popular perception of witchcraft in pre-modern Europe" \[^11\]. The 15th- and 16th-century explosion of witch imagery was not decoration of the witch hunt but constitutive of it: "images of witchcraft and sorcery printed in pamphlets and other visual sources during the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries transformed and sensationalised the narrative of witchcraft and its dangers"; the creators of the new images functioned as "mediators between the elite world of theology and demonological theory on the one hand, and popular perceptions of unseen dangers in everyday life on the other" \[^11\]. The iconography incorporated "themes not previously prominent in discussions of witchcraft and not necessarily related to one another" — including "male fantasies about female power, superstitions attributed to non-Christian peoples, and ideas about New World cannibalism" — and Zika argues, "very convincingly, that such transformations contributed to a wider acceptance of witch-belief in the sixteenth century and underpinned persecutions during that period" \[^11\]. His larger thesis: the images "helped to fuse demonological theory and theology with contemporary (that is, sixteenth-century) perceptions of medicine, of sexuality and gender, and of peasant belief" \[^11\].

Zika is, in a very direct sense, the missing partner to Federici for the primary period. Federici argues that the witch hunt was constitutive of primitive accumulation \[^12\]\[^13\]; Zika argues that the witch \*image\* was constitutive of the witch hunt \[^11\]. Put the two together and one has a materialist iconography of the witch — the female sign of demonic threat as a piece of ideological infrastructure that the hunt required in order to run. Federici cites parts of Zika's milieu but does not fully take on the visual-culture argument. The present essay's move — the extension of that materialist iconography from the \*witch\* (Zika's object) to the \*civic Maiden\* (Warner's object) — is a logical continuation of what Zika did for the primary period. If the witch hunt had to travel on images to be thinkable, so, perhaps, did the state that later put the disciplined female form on its money.

\*\*A taxonomy.\*\* The predecessors above fall into three groups, and it is worth naming them precisely so the essay's own contribution can be located.

\- \*Materialist iconography without Federici's reproductive-labour thesis.\* Doy is the paradigm case. She has class analysis, revolution, and the female sign as class artefact; she does not have the witch hunt as constitutive violence of gender.  
\- \*Federician analysis without civic allegory.\* The "witching of art history" current — Deepwell, Naessens, Kokkinen, Turley-Sonne — and Kozomora on ex-Yugoslav contemporary art \[^4\]\[^5\]\[^6\]\[^7\]\[^3\]. They read the witch through Federici; they do not read the civic Maiden.  
\- \*Methodological infrastructure.\* Mansoor demonstrates the method (iconography whose substrate is primitive accumulation); Vishmidt sharpens the theoretical hygiene around "the body"; Zika supplies the archival-visual-culture argument for the primary period. None of them applies the resulting apparatus to Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, or Columbia.

The essay's proposed contribution sits in the intersection of the three: Federician analysis (with the reproductive-labour thesis intact), of Warner's canonical objects (civic female allegory on the coin and the pediment), using Mansoor's methodological example and Zika's kind of visual-culture argument as the model.

\#\# IX. Where the bridge is still missing

The catalogue above establishes that the traffic between Warner and Federici is real and has produced a decade or more of interesting work. It also establishes what has not been done. This section names the specific gap that Sections IV–VII were attempting to fill.

\*\*A sustained materialist iconography of female civic allegory proper.\*\* The Warnerian object is a very specific thing: the abstract civic Maiden — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia, Lady Liberty — on the coin, the seal, the courthouse pediment, the national frontispiece. It is \*not\* the witch (Zika, the "witching" current), \*not\* the maternal-biographical figure (Kozomora on the ex-Yugoslav nation-body is closest, but her objects are contemporary artists, not the historical civic allegories), \*not\* the class-marked female figure of revolutionary or Communard imagery (Doy is closest, but she does not have Federici's mechanism). The cleanly Warnerian civic allegory has not, so far as this essay's search could determine, received a sustained Federician treatment.

Doy comes closest for revolutionary France — her chapter on "women and the bourgeois revolution of 1789" is, effectively, the class analysis of the political-female moment in which Marianne consolidates \[^2\]. But Doy is pre-\*Caliban\*; the specific Federician mechanism (witch hunt as constitutive violence, reproductive labour as capitalism's hidden abode) is not in her toolkit. A reading of Marianne that combined Doy's class-of-1789 with Federici's reproductive-labour thesis would produce something like Section IV; nobody had done it.

Kozomora comes closest for the nation-body — her formulation of women as "metaphoric markers of the national body, a symbolic battlefield" during the ex-Yugoslav transition is the argument this essay is trying to run on Britannia and Columbia, on a different accumulation and a different century \[^3\]. But her objects are contemporary artists working through the ex-Yugoslav witch, not the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century civic allegories through which the modern nation-state consolidated.

The "witching" literature comes closest for the mechanism — the reclamation of the witch as an anti-capitalist emblem, worked through contemporary artistic practice \[^5\]\[^6\]\[^7\]. But it is committed to the witch as its object. The civic Maiden — the icon whose function is precisely to be \*not\* the witch, to be the cleaned-up state form the witch was killed to produce — is outside its frame.

\*\*The structural argument has no developed application.\*\* The Goux/Cady general-equivalents argument that Section III made central is, on the essay's search, the closest theoretical scaffolding for a materialist iconography of civic female allegory \[^14\]. It has not, so far as could be determined, been pulled through to developed readings of specific icons. Cady applies it to money and the female body in early modern literature; Goux applies it to general semiotics. Neither takes the next step to Marianne on the franc, Britannia on the pound, Justitia on the courthouse pediment, or Columbia on the American medal. The essay's sections IV–VII are attempts to do exactly that.

\*\*Zika's argument has not been extended forward in time.\*\* Zika's demonstration that the witch hunt travelled on images — that the visual culture of demonology was constitutive of the persecution, not decorative of it — is the model for a materialist iconography of the primary period \[^11\]. The obvious next question, and the one this essay is asking, is whether the state that consolidated \*after\* the witch hunt travelled on images in the same way — whether the disciplined female form on the coin and the pediment is doing analogous ideological work at analogous scale for the mature capitalist state. Zika has not asked the question; nobody working in his tradition has, either.

\*\*The Warner–Federici comparison has not been staged head-on.\*\* This is the smallest but most striking gap. The two authors are frequently cited together — Warner as the iconographic reference for the female civic sign, Federici as the political-economic reference for the female body under capital — but the essay's search located no paper that stages the comparison in its own right. Section II attempted the comparison. The predecessors closest to that operation (Kozomora, Doy) do half of it: Kozomora holds both frames at once but on a contemporary object; Doy operates on the historical object but does not have Federici's mechanism. Nobody, so far as could be found, has run the comparison directly.

\*\*A note on completeness.\*\* Two disclaimers close this section. First, the search that produced these predecessors was a paper-and-web search, not an archival search of monographs and edited volumes, where synthetic work of the kind proposed here is more likely to live — Kokoli's \*The Feminist Uncanny\*, work coming out of \*Third Text\*, exhibition catalogues from shows like \*Witch Hunt\* or \*Magic Women\*. The gap this essay is describing may be smaller in book-length scholarship than in the journal record. Second, the absence of a paper that stages an argument is not proof that the argument is right. It is only evidence that the argument has not been staged. The essay's proposed contribution is a synthesis of two adjacent literatures rather than a review of an existing one — which is both its opportunity and its exposure. Section XI returns to that exposure in the caveats.

Section X pulls the four case studies back together as a single template — Marianne / enclosure, Britannia / empire, Justitia / the state form, Columbia / settler colonialism — and states the payoff the essay has been building toward: the female civic allegory as the post-mortem mask of the witch, engraved on the instrument of the general equivalent, resolving in capital's favour the conflict Warner and Federici together let us see.
