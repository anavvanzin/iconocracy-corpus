# Fetishized Form and Explicit Legal Violence: A Unified Framework

# Fetishized Form and Explicit Legal Violence: A Unified Framework for Legal Iconography, Codification, and Capital Accumulation

## Why this framework

Across the earlier exchanges, three seemingly distinct objects kept returning to the same underlying structure: the female allegorical body on courtroom pediments and coinage, the nineteenth-century civil, penal, and colonial codes that expropriated women's legal capacity, and the theoretical apparatus (Pashukanis, Federici) available to interpret them. The most economical framework capable of holding all three under one analytic roof is Guilherme Leite Gonçalves' reformulation of capitalist accumulation as a two-sided process: **the exchange of equivalents and expropriation**, corresponding to two distinct modes of legal operation — **fetishized form** and **explicit legal violence** <citations>gj3SectA:1-3</citations>.

This document sets out the framework, positions Pashukanis and Federici as its two theoretical wings, shows how legal iconography and nineteenth-century codification map onto it, and argues that this formulation is the most directly usable frame for the ICONOCRACIA project.

## 1. The core proposition

Gonçalves' central claim rejects a monist theory of law under capitalism. Capital accumulation is "constituted of two contradictory and entangled sides: the exchange of equivalents and expropriation," and any theory that grasps only one side misdescribes the whole <citations>gj3SectA:1-2</citations>. The Pashukanian critique of legal form "is limited to examining the position of law within the framework of the exchange of equivalents, of [legal] form and of commodity fetishism"; but capitalism in its expansionist phase depends on the commodification of "spaces not yet commodified," which requires "open, non-fetishized violence capable of imposing capitalist social relations on territories and groups" — and in that phase "law appears as explicit legal violence that blatantly prescribes social inequality" <citations>gj3SectA:2-3,gj3SectA:13</citations>.

The framework therefore posits two coordinates:

| Coordinate | Legal mode | Sphere | Theoretical wing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exchange of equivalents | Fetishized legal form (formal equality, abstract subject, contract) | Circulation | Pashukanis |
| Expropriation | Explicit legal violence (statutory coercion, codified inequality) | Reproduction, dispossession | Federici |

These are not two kinds of law but two registers of the same legal order. In its methodological form: Pashukanian critique "offers a solution" to liberal-Habermasian legal idealism "by recognizing that the 'ought to be' is already realized in the structures of inequality," but it cannot exhaust the analysis of law because "it limits itself to grasping the position of law only at the moment of commodity exchange" <citations>UFspHLhg:2-3,UFspHLhg:12</citations>. Beyond that moment, capitalism operates through "legal discourses of othering (human rights), regimes of privatization (public-private partnerships) and criminal law (criminalization of protest and poverty)" — objects the commodity-form account alone cannot theorize <citations>UFspHLhg:7</citations>.

## 2. The Pashukanian wing: fetishized form

Pashukanis' *General Theory of Law and Marxism* derives the legal form from the commodity: bourgeois law is "characterised by an emphasis on the legal form — as distinct from both its content (particular legal rules) and its function (reproducing or maintaining capitalism)," treats the legal form as "an historically specific form of regulating social relations," and "builds upon Marx's theory of the commodity to theorise the legal form, formal legal equality, and the basic categories of private and public law under capitalism" <citations>wXhKKUfg:1-3</citations>. What needs explaining is not that law serves the ruling class (instrumentalism) but that it appears as neutral, universal, and equal: Pashukanis' contribution is a "materialist account of the *form* of law itself" that analyses "such concepts as 'legal rule,' 'legal person,' and so on, which, it seems, can be taken in abstraction from any specific content" <citations>T1RNvBGw:14,T1RNvBGw:17</citations>.

For our purposes, the crucial move is Pashukanis' extension of Marx's fetishism to law: the legal subject is the personification of the commodity-owner, and formal equality is the ideological face of exchange. This is what allows the framework to say something specific about legal *iconography*. The allegorical female figure — Justitia, Liberty, Marianne, the Republic — is the fetishized form made visible. The abstract, sexless-yet-female body is the pictorial correlate of the abstract subject; particular embodied women cannot occupy that place without breaking the formal-equality conceit. This is why the courthouse and the coin converge on the same iconographic vocabulary: both are surfaces on which the fetishized form of exchange presents itself as eternal.

## 3. The Federician wing: explicit legal violence

Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* supplies what Pashukanis systematically omits. Where Pashukanis theorises the circulation-side legal form, Federici reconstructs the production- and reproduction-side violence: enclosure, witch hunts, criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy, expulsion of women from waged trades, and the codification of the patriarchal household. Law shows up not as fetish but as coercion. This is the terrain on which Roberts generalises the argument: capitalism has "always been underpinned by the use of state power to discursively construct and materially manage" populations marginalised by capitalist deepening, and "the law, along with social welfare regimes, have operated in ways that are highly gendered" <citations>fuvvmzhQ:0-2</citations>.

The Federician wing supplies two things the Pashukanian wing lacks: a theory of *primitive accumulation as ongoing* rather than pre-historical, and a theory of *gender* as constitutive rather than incidental. Wilén, defending Pashukanis, concedes the standing of "objections against Pashukanis's focus on the sphere of circulation at the cost of production, his exclusion of inequalities of race and gender" — even while arguing they can be answered within a reconstructed form-analysis <citations>bke1XFMQ:6</citations>. Whether one takes the reconstructive route (Wilén, Dimick) or the additive one (Gonçalves, Fletcher), the substantive terrain Federici occupies is not optional.

## 4. The bridge literatures

Three recent bodies of work operate on the seam between the two wings and are essential to a working framework rather than a schematic one.

**Ruth Fletcher's** legal-form-plus-reproduction project accepts Pashukanis' method and extends it: "for Pashukanis, legal form constitutes subjects as if they have property rights over objects, generates exchange value, and represents differently situated subjects as if they are equal," and the task is to "develop… legal form analysis so as to accommodate the roles of social reproduction and consumption in the generation of care value and use value in commodity-exchanging societies" <citations>FZaE_LdQ:2,FZaE_LdQ:4</citations>. This is the friendliest possible amendment: it retains Pashukanis' derivation but asks what happens when the form is applied to labour that is not, or is only ambiguously, commodified.

**Portella and Busk** propose to reconstruct patriarchy through Marx's distinction between formal and real subsumption, so that capitalism is understood as both appropriating antecedent patriarchal forms and producing "new forms of gendered exploitation and oppression" <citations>No-WBz5w:0-1,No-WBz5w:3-4</citations>. This move addresses one of the standing objections to Federici — that her account risks granting patriarchy a transhistorical autonomy that is then "captured" by capital.

**Onur Ulas Ince** proposes to dissolve the Pashukanis/Federici tension by reformulating primitive accumulation itself as "the coercive capitalization of social relations of reproduction," expressed through the pairing of "capital-positing violence and capital-preserving violence" and drawing on Benjamin's law-making/law-preserving distinction <citations>dDHZG6Xg:3-6</citations>. Ince puts gendered expropriation and the legal form within a single theory of *capitalist juridical violence* rather than two.

None of these three fully replaces Gonçalves' formulation, but each supplies operative content for the seam between fetishized form and explicit legal violence.

## 5. Iconography as the fetishized side made visible

The nineteenth-century turn to allegorical female figures on courthouse pediments, republican monuments, and national coinage is legible within the framework as the *visualisation* of the fetishized side. Jeandrée's argument is that after the French Revolution, sovereignty "is no longer an absolute model of divine authority but has turned into a contingent image of itself," so the dispute about the "symbolic form of the social" becomes the very essence of modern democracy, and the visual arts face the new task of depicting authority "in a society where the ultimate place of power is empty" <citations>zP_37xcQ:1-3</citations>. Female allegory is what fills that empty place — not as a claim about women, but as the available pictorial currency for a new political order in which the sovereign body has been abstracted.

Huygebaert's study of nineteenth-century Belgian legal iconography catches the structural irony: women are abundant on the walls and absent from the bench, and these sculpted and painted figures "represented not uncommonly the very core of what justice aimed to be" in a male-dominated profession <citations>LtoEkYSA:0-1</citations>. Resnik and Curtis read this cross-culturally: nations rely on "a statue of a large hulking woman" with scales and sword to claim that their justice systems aspire to fairness and to lay claim to power <citations>pM1iHRAA:0-1</citations>. Wardle, drawing on Žižek, gives the ideological reading: by personifying justice "in the style of ancient gods and angels," Lady Justice casts human-made legal concepts in "an eternal and cosmic cloak, making laws integral to systemic exploitation and oppression less likely to be challenged" and materialises formal-procedural justice as a belief object that "relies on metaphysics to cast the privileged and persecuted as equal under law" <citations>xn-6gTOQ:3-4</citations>.

The migration onto money completes the pattern. Kalba's reading of Roty's *La Semeuse* on the French silver franc argues that the agrarian female motif is "far more compatible with the financial industry, where value is realized on and through paper, than its agricultural motif appears to suggest," opening "the putatively transactional realm of economics" to aesthetic-discursive practices that helped naturalise fiat value <citations>pSYfYbOA:1-2</citations>. What Justitia does for the abstraction of law, the Sower does for the abstraction of value. Both are the fetishized form appearing as an eternal and bodily image.

This is precisely what Pashukanis' theory predicts and what Federici's theory alone cannot explain: *why the fetish takes this particular visual form*. The answer runs through the abstract subject.

## 6. Codification as the expropriative side made statutory

The nineteenth-century codification programs — civil, penal, poor-law, prostitution-regulation, protective-labour, and colonial personal-law — are the explicit-legal-violence side made statutory. Each program does specific work in the reproduction of the gendered substrate the formal-equal subject stands on.

The **civil codes** lock the male-headed household into private law. The Code Napoléon of 1804 is "the first bourgeois law book based on principles of liberty, equality and security of personal property" and simultaneously "characterized by rigid regulations reinforcing male dominance," with marriage and family provisions that "profoundly influenced the daily lives and agency of women" <citations>xG7y6QNw:0,xG7y6QNw:2-3</citations>. The common-law parallel is Blackstone's coverture, which Stretton and Kesselring show was extended and retooled, not relaxed, across the nineteenth century: "generations of judges, legislators, and husbands invoked coverture to limit married women's rights and property" <citations>ZXDUHfxQ:0-2</citations>. The Brazilian Civil Code of 1916 codified "the nuclear-family model of husband and wife" while retaining "the asymmetry of private power relations in which men prevailed over their wives," a settlement that "implied severe restrictions on the civil rights of married women, a situation only mitigated in 1962" <citations>-rwhBZ_g:4-10</citations>.

**Enclosure and the criminalisation of gleaning** strip women's customary access to subsistence. Humphries shows that "women and children were the primary exploiters of common rights" and that the loss of the commons therefore pushed households into wage-dependence <citations>myLyOsyQ:0,myLyOsyQ:2</citations>. Qiu's study of Essex 1830–1890 tracks the second wave in judicial practice: gleaning "was increasingly criminalized" as "courts progressively prioritized landowners' claims over customary access," with rulings that "oscillated between recognizing its economic necessity and reinforcing property rights, ultimately undermining poor women's claims" <citations>JGYguhfA:1,JGYguhfA:5-7</citations>. Qiu argues explicitly that the gleaning crisis "exemplifies the intersection of economic dispossession, legal marginalization, and gendered discipline" <citations>JGYguhfA:8</citations>.

**Poor Law and vagrancy** convert that dispossession into a disciplinary wage-or-workhouse choice. Levine-Clark shows the 1834 New Poor Law "placed women in a difficult position by forcing them to decide whether they were women or workers," simultaneously assuming them "physically fragile dependents of male providers" and treating any "able-bodied petitioner" as one who must support themselves through gainful employment <citations>qYLpaMSw:0-2</citations>.

**Reproductive criminalisation** captures women's reproductive labour for state and medical authority. Roth's work on Rio 1890–1940 finds that "modernizing states began criminalizing practices like abortion," and that "early-twentieth-century Brazil experienced a shift from familial to state patriarchy during which women's sexual honor — and reproduction — became public goods" through the 1890 Penal Code, obstetric professionalisation, and police expansion <citations>4lEESeGg:1,4lEESeGg:3-7</citations>. Backhouse's Canadian study shows the same pattern was driven not by public opinion but by "a determined group of male physicians who wished to assert monopoly control over a profession they were struggling to create" <citations>kpVU-PlQ:1,kpVU-PlQ:3</citations>.

**The Contagious Diseases Acts** assert direct sovereignty over working-class and colonised women's bodies. Tripathi shows the Indian CD Acts "specifically targeted women involved in prostitution, largely ignoring the role of men" and "introduced Victorian moral values into Indian law, aimed at controlling female sexuality and venereal diseases among British troops" <citations>vriU6GYw:3,vriU6GYw:5</citations>.

**Protective labour legislation** produces a permitted-but-subordinated female wage. Malone finds English protective law between 1830 and 1914 structured by a "discourse of danger" in which "diverse claims" all converged on "the idea that women's work outside the home was dangerous to society and required state intervention" <citations>Hu0UjTEA:7-9</citations>.

**Colonial personal-law codification** exports and intensifies the whole structure. Agnes argues that in the Indian Presidencies the introduction of "a legal structure modelled on English courts" combined "with the benevolence of the English jurists, and the worst distortions of Brahminical smriti texts" to usher in "the patriarchal modernity of India" <citations>ovW4Zzwg:2-3</citations>.

Each program is a specific statutory or jurisprudential instantiation of what Gonçalves calls the expropriative side; together they are the nineteenth-century legal infrastructure of primitive accumulation.

Federici explains why actual women had to be absent from the bench, the polling station, the guild, and the parish vestry at the same moment Justitia was being installed on the facade: the substrate the formal-equal subject stands on is produced and maintained by explicit legal violence exercised on women's bodies, labour, and reproductive capacity. The pairing has an additional virtue: it lets one *date* both sides together. The period at which nineteenth-century codifications are most fiercely contested — the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882, the CD Acts repeal of 1886, the Brazilian Civil Code debates of 1901–1916 — is also the period at which Justitia is most heavily deployed on courthouse pediments and the Republic most heavily engraved on national coinage. This is not coincidence. It is the same operation seen from its two sides, contested at the same historical moment because it is one order under strain.

## 8. Application to the ICONOCRACIA problem

For the ICONOCRACIA project — visual sovereignty in Latin America, Brazilian legal codification, and the political economy of state-formation — Gonçalves' formulation has three specific advantages over its rivals.

First, it is *Latin-American originated*: Gonçalves' theoretical work is developed in and for the analytic problems of the Brazilian and Latin American periphery, where the standard European-derived accounts of primitive accumulation and legal form struggle to hold together the post-abolition reorganisation of labour, the extension of formal citizenship to the abstract subject, and the constitutive exclusions of race and gender that condition that extension. Andermann's volume argues that in Latin America visual culture carried unusually heavy weight in producing nation-state consent — literacy was restricted, so the iconographic apparatus did more of the legitimation work — and that the iconography of the state-form "has so far escaped serious critical treatment" <citations>9T39Z-0w:0-2</citations>. Gonçalves' framework is designed to hold that iconographic apparatus and the codification programs (Brazilian Penal Code 1890; Civil Code 1916) within one analytic system.

Second, it *avoids the false choice* between Pashukanis and Federici. The framework does not ask whether legal form or gendered expropriation is primary; it asks how each side of accumulation operates in a given historical conjuncture and how they interlock. This is the correct question for a project whose object — the Republic on the coin, Justitia on the courthouse, the coverture provision, the abortion criminalisation, the racial reorganisation of labour after 1888 — occupies both sides at once.

Third, it *makes iconography theoretically continuous with codification* rather than parallel to it. In the framework, the female allegorical body is not a supplementary cultural object to be explained by reference to legal history; it is the visible surface of the fetishized side of the very same legal order whose expropriative side is the statutory apparatus. Iconography and codification stand as two coordinates of one political-economic system. This is precisely the analytic move ICONOCRACIA needs to make.

## 9. Where the framework remains contested

Three real caveats.

The **most orthodox Pashukanians** (Wilén, Dimick) argue that form-analysis can be extended to gender and race without a Federician supplement — that the commodity-form approach is more flexible than its feminist critics suppose <citations>bke1XFMQ:5-6</citations>. If they are right, Gonçalves' two-sided formulation collapses back into a single (reconstructed) Pashukanian theory. Whether Fletcher's extension of form-analysis to social reproduction <citations>FZaE_LdQ:2,FZaE_LdQ:4</citations> succeeds on Pashukanian terms is an open question.

**Federici herself** has been pressed on the theoretical status of patriarchy — whether her account effectively grants it transhistorical autonomy that is then captured by capital, the "origins-subsistence" reading Portella and Busk push back against <citations>No-WBz5w:0-1,No-WBz5w:3</citations>. Gonçalves' framework inherits this problem to the extent that it takes the expropriative side to be gendered "all the way down."

**The nineteenth-century codification arc is not monotonic.** The same Justitia icon that legitimised the codes was being repurposed against them by suffragists; the Married Women's Property Acts and the CD Acts repeal are within the century. Any application of the framework that presents the two sides as smoothly interlocking will underdescribe the contestation that runs through both — a point that itself follows from the framework, since a two-sided order under strain will be contested on both sides.

None of these caveats defeats the framework; each specifies where its application requires care.

## 10. Summary

The most viable framework for holding legal iconography, visual sovereignty, and nineteenth-century codification within one analytic system is Gonçalves' reformulation of capitalist accumulation as exchange plus expropriation, mapped onto the two legal modes of fetishized form and explicit legal violence. Pashukanis theorises the fetishized side; Federici the expropriative side; Fletcher, Portella and Busk, and Ince operate on the seam. Applied to the nineteenth century, the framework treats the allegorical female body on the courthouse pediment and coin, and the coverture provision, gleaning prosecution, abortion criminalisation, CD Act, protective labour statute, and colonial personal-law code, as two registers of a single legal apparatus. Applied to the ICONOCRACIA problem specifically, it provides a Latin-American originated, non-choice, iconography-continuous frame that treats the Republic on the coin and the Civil Code of 1916 as coordinates of one system rather than as separate objects.
