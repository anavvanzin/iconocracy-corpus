# ICONOCRACIA — Consolidated Dossier

## Legal iconography, visual sovereignty, and the political economy of capitalist accumulation

This dossier gathers the full research trajectory into a single deliverable. It sets out the operative framework (Gonçalves' two-sided theory of accumulation, with Pashukanis and Federici as its wings and Fletcher, Portella & Busk, and Ince at the seam), embeds the accompanying timeline figure spanning 1480–1920, and closes with the annotated source corpus and full bibliography. All quotations retain their citation tags; the papers-table sibling artifact (`iconocracia-source-corpus.yaml`) lists every source with its lane assignment and role in the argument.

## Part I — The framework

### 1. Why this framework

Across the ICONOCRACIA exchanges, three seemingly distinct objects kept returning to the same underlying structure: the female allegorical body on courtroom pediments and coinage, the nineteenth-century civil, penal, and colonial codes that expropriated women's legal capacity, and the theoretical apparatus (Pashukanis, Federici) available to interpret them. The most economical framework capable of holding all three under one analytic roof is Guilherme Leite Gonçalves' reformulation of capitalist accumulation as a two-sided process: **the exchange of equivalents and expropriation**, corresponding to two distinct modes of legal operation — **fetishized form** and **explicit legal violence** <citations>gj3SectA:1-3</citations>.

### 2. The core proposition

Gonçalves rejects a monist theory of law under capitalism. Capital accumulation is "constituted of two contradictory and entangled sides: the exchange of equivalents and expropriation," and any theory that grasps only one side misdescribes the whole <citations>gj3SectA:1-2</citations>. The Pashukanian critique of legal form "is limited to examining the position of law within the framework of the exchange of equivalents, of [legal] form and of commodity fetishism"; but capitalism in its expansionist phase depends on the commodification of "spaces not yet commodified," which requires "open, non-fetishized violence capable of imposing capitalist social relations on territories and groups" — and in that phase "law appears as explicit legal violence that blatantly prescribes social inequality" <citations>gj3SectA:2-3,gj3SectA:13</citations>.

The framework therefore posits two coordinates:

| Coordinate | Legal mode | Sphere | Theoretical wing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exchange of equivalents | Fetishized legal form (formal equality, abstract subject, contract) | Circulation | Pashukanis |
| Expropriation | Explicit legal violence (statutory coercion, codified inequality) | Reproduction, dispossession | Federici |

These are not two kinds of law but two registers of the same legal order. Pashukanian critique "offers a solution" to liberal-Habermasian legal idealism "by recognizing that the 'ought to be' is already realized in the structures of inequality," but it cannot exhaust the analysis of law because "it limits itself to grasping the position of law only at the moment of commodity exchange" <citations>UFspHLhg:2-3,UFspHLhg:12</citations>. Beyond that moment, capitalism operates through "legal discourses of othering (human rights), regimes of privatization (public-private partnerships) and criminal law (criminalization of protest and poverty)" — objects the commodity-form account alone cannot theorize <citations>UFspHLhg:7</citations>.

### 3. The Pashukanian wing: fetishized form

Pashukanis' *General Theory of Law and Marxism* derives the legal form from the commodity: bourgeois law is "characterised by an emphasis on the legal form — as distinct from both its content (particular legal rules) and its function (reproducing or maintaining capitalism)," treats the legal form as "an historically specific form of regulating social relations," and "builds upon Marx's theory of the commodity to theorise the legal form, formal legal equality, and the basic categories of private and public law under capitalism" <citations>wXhKKUfg:1-3</citations>. What needs explaining is not that law serves the ruling class (instrumentalism) but that it appears as neutral, universal, and equal: Pashukanis' contribution is a "materialist account of the *form* of law itself" that analyses "such concepts as 'legal rule,' 'legal person,' and so on, which, it seems, can be taken in abstraction from any specific content" <citations>T1RNvBGw:14,T1RNvBGw:17</citations>.

The crucial move for our purposes is Pashukanis' extension of Marx's fetishism to law: the legal subject is the personification of the commodity-owner, and formal equality is the ideological face of exchange. This is what allows the framework to say something specific about legal *iconography*. The allegorical female figure — Justitia, Liberty, Marianne, the Republic — is the fetishized form made visible.

### 4. The Federician wing: explicit legal violence

Federici's *Caliban and the Witch* supplies what Pashukanis systematically omits. Where Pashukanis theorises the circulation-side legal form, Federici reconstructs the production- and reproduction-side violence: enclosure, witch hunts, criminalisation of women's reproductive autonomy, expulsion of women from waged trades, and the codification of the patriarchal household. Law shows up not as fetish but as coercion. Roberts generalises the argument: capitalism has "always been underpinned by the use of state power to discursively construct and materially manage" populations marginalised by capitalist deepening, and "the law, along with social welfare regimes, have operated in ways that are highly gendered" <citations>fuvvmzhQ:0-2</citations>.

Wilén, defending Pashukanis, concedes the standing of "objections against Pashukanis's focus on the sphere of circulation at the cost of production, his exclusion of inequalities of race and gender" — even while arguing they can be answered within a reconstructed form-analysis <citations>bke1XFMQ:6</citations>.

### 5. The bridge literatures

Three recent bodies of work operate on the seam between the two wings.

**Fletcher's** legal-form-plus-reproduction project accepts Pashukanis' method and extends it: "for Pashukanis, legal form constitutes subjects as if they have property rights over objects, generates exchange value, and represents differently situated subjects as if they are equal," and the task is to "develop… legal form analysis so as to accommodate the roles of social reproduction and consumption in the generation of care value and use value in commodity-exchanging societies" <citations>FZaE_LdQ:2,FZaE_LdQ:4</citations>.

**Portella and Busk** propose to reconstruct patriarchy through Marx's distinction between formal and real subsumption, so that capitalism is understood as both appropriating antecedent patriarchal forms and producing "new forms of gendered exploitation and oppression" <citations>No-WBz5w:0-1,No-WBz5w:3-4</citations>.

**Ince** proposes to dissolve the Pashukanis/Federici tension by reformulating primitive accumulation itself as "the coercive capitalization of social relations of reproduction," expressed through the pairing of "capital-positing violence and capital-preserving violence" and drawing on Benjamin's law-making/law-preserving distinction <citations>dDHZG6Xg:3-6</citations>.

## Part II — Iconography as the fetishized side made visible

Jeandrée's argument is that after the French Revolution, sovereignty "is no longer an absolute model of divine authority but has turned into a contingent image of itself," so the dispute about the "symbolic form of the social" becomes the very essence of modern democracy, and the visual arts face the new task of depicting authority "in a society where the ultimate place of power is empty" <citations>zP_37xcQ:1-3</citations>. Female allegory is what fills that empty place — not as a claim about women, but as the available pictorial currency for a new political order in which the sovereign body has been abstracted. Agulhon establishes the genealogy: the revolutionary half-draped female Liberty is drawn directly from the Renaissance allegorical vocabulary, and once the Republic is installed she "becomes the symbol of the bourgeois state" <citations>roFMEGQQ:1-4</citations>.

Huygebaert's study of nineteenth-century Belgian legal iconography catches the structural irony: women are abundant on the walls and absent from the bench, and these sculpted and painted figures "represented not uncommonly the very core of what justice aimed to be" in a male-dominated profession <citations>LtoEkYSA:0-1</citations>. Resnik and Curtis read this cross-culturally: nations rely on "a statue of a large hulking woman" with scales and sword to claim that their justice systems aspire to fairness and to lay claim to power <citations>pM1iHRAA:0-1</citations>. Wardle, drawing on Žižek, gives the ideological reading: by personifying justice "in the style of ancient gods and angels," Lady Justice casts human-made legal concepts in "an eternal and cosmic cloak, making laws integral to systemic exploitation and oppression less likely to be challenged" and materialises formal-procedural justice as a belief object that "relies on metaphysics to cast the privileged and persecuted as equal under law" <citations>xn-6gTOQ:3-4</citations>. Goodrich, working historically from the sixteenth-century emblem books, calls Justitia an *aenigma iuris* — a legal symbol whose referent has been forgotten <citations>PKGnq9ww:3</citations>.

The migration onto money completes the pattern. Hewitt notes that for more than two centuries paper money worldwide has carried neo-classical female allegories <citations>pc_eZ49g:2</citations>. Kalba's reading of Roty's *La Semeuse* on the French silver franc argues that the agrarian female motif is "far more compatible with the financial industry, where value is realized on and through paper, than its agricultural motif appears to suggest," opening "the putatively transactional realm of economics" to aesthetic-discursive practices that helped naturalise fiat value <citations>pSYfYbOA:1-2</citations>. Hawkes' study of the seventeenth-century English transition shows that economic, liturgical, and magical modes of representation had not been fully distinguished at the start of the period, and were often connected on the grounds that they were all forms of alienated labour <citations>BJfkKovA:3-5</citations>. What Justitia does for the abstraction of law, the Sower does for the abstraction of value.

## Part III — Codification as the expropriative side made statutory

The nineteenth-century codification programs are the explicit-legal-violence side made statutory.

**Civil codes.** The Code Napoléon of 1804 is "the first bourgeois law book based on principles of liberty, equality and security of personal property" and simultaneously "characterized by rigid regulations reinforcing male dominance," with marriage and family provisions that "profoundly influenced the daily lives and agency of women" <citations>xG7y6QNw:0,xG7y6QNw:2-3</citations>. The common-law parallel is Blackstone's coverture, which Stretton and Kesselring show was extended and retooled, not relaxed, across the nineteenth century: "generations of judges, legislators, and husbands invoked coverture to limit married women's rights and property" <citations>ZXDUHfxQ:0-2</citations>. The Brazilian Civil Code of 1916 codified "the nuclear-family model of husband and wife" while retaining "the asymmetry of private power relations in which men prevailed over their wives," a settlement that "implied severe restrictions on the civil rights of married women, a situation only mitigated in 1962" <citations>-rwhBZ_g:4-10</citations>.

**Enclosure and gleaning.** Humphries shows that "women and children were the primary exploiters of common rights" and that the loss of the commons pushed households into wage-dependence <citations>myLyOsyQ:0,myLyOsyQ:2</citations>. Qiu's study of Essex 1830–1890 tracks the second wave in judicial practice: gleaning "was increasingly criminalized" as "courts progressively prioritized landowners' claims over customary access," with rulings that "oscillated between recognizing its economic necessity and reinforcing property rights, ultimately undermining poor women's claims" <citations>JGYguhfA:1,JGYguhfA:5-7</citations>. The gleaning crisis "exemplifies the intersection of economic dispossession, legal marginalization, and gendered discipline" <citations>JGYguhfA:8</citations>.

**Poor law and vagrancy.** Levine-Clark shows the 1834 New Poor Law "placed women in a difficult position by forcing them to decide whether they were women or workers," simultaneously assuming them "physically fragile dependents of male providers" and treating any "able-bodied petitioner" as one who must support themselves through gainful employment <citations>qYLpaMSw:0-2</citations>. Crossman finds the same in the Irish Poor Law, where workhouse classification was "predicated upon perceptions of female sexuality" <citations>1HL6Qqlg:2-3</citations>. Poutanen shows Montreal vagrancy law functioning as gendered moral regulation that translated "bourgeois notions of industry, sobriety, and discipline" into criminal sanction <citations>mg3XrI3A:11-13</citations>.

**Reproductive criminalisation.** Roth on Rio 1890–1940: "modernizing states began criminalizing practices like abortion," and "early-twentieth-century Brazil experienced a shift from familial to state patriarchy during which women's sexual honor — and reproduction — became public goods" <citations>4lEESeGg:1,4lEESeGg:3-7</citations>. Backhouse's Canadian study shows the same pattern was driven by "a determined group of male physicians who wished to assert monopoly control over a profession they were struggling to create" <citations>kpVU-PlQ:1,kpVU-PlQ:3</citations>.

**Contagious Diseases Acts.** Tripathi on the Indian CD Acts: they "specifically targeted women involved in prostitution, largely ignoring the role of men" and "introduced Victorian moral values into Indian law, aimed at controlling female sexuality and venereal diseases among British troops" <citations>vriU6GYw:3,vriU6GYw:5</citations>. Levine reads the apparatus as an explicitly territorial sovereignty over working-class and colonised women's bodies <citations>XPFM02Cg:5</citations>.

**Protective labour.** Malone finds English protective law between 1830 and 1914 structured by a "discourse of danger" converging on "the idea that women's work outside the home was dangerous to society and required state intervention" <citations>Hu0UjTEA:7-9</citations>.

**Colonial personal law.** Agnes argues that in the Indian Presidencies the introduction of "a legal structure modelled on English courts" combined "with the benevolence of the English jurists, and the worst distortions of Brahminical smriti texts" to usher in "the patriarchal modernity of India" <citations>ovW4Zzwg:2-3</citations>. Sreenivas shows in the joint-family property disputes that "property relations within families… depended on women's inequality" <citations>xgz9lKXA:1,xgz9lKXA:3</citations>. Reis' work on Brazilian patriarchy is where the European frame requires its biggest qualification: the elite project of "civilization compatible with slavery" disciplined *white aristocratic women* into civilized comportment to manage slave relations, while simultaneously stereotyping and criminalising Black enslaved women <citations>1Esq7UWw:1,1Esq7UWw:3-4</citations>.

**Early-modern penal substrate.** Behind the nineteenth-century codifications lies the early-modern legal-demonological apparatus: the *Malleus Maleficarum* of 1487 <citations>3AafpZHA:0-2,e0SrgtyA:0-2</citations>, the *Constitutio Criminalis Carolina* of 1532 <citations>rI-bPrfw:0-8</citations>, the Tudor–Stuart English witch statutes (1563, 1604, with the Jacobean Infanticide Act of 1624) <citations>HiRKD7pw:3-5,fBP8zeMg:14,fBP8zeMg:16,J28JMywg:0</citations>, the Bamberg and Würzburg mass trials of 1626–31 <citations>gvThKkrQ:0-4,nOCSCrSg:0-2</citations>, the Hopkins East Anglian trials of 1645–47 <citations>J0uxJrDg:0-2</citations>, the Scottish peak of 1661–62 <citations>uJNuB-4A:7</citations>, and Salem in 1692 <citations>V_n3UOvg:1</citations>. The decriminalisation arc runs Louis XIV's Paris 1682 <citations>Si-vRwbA:1-4</citations> and the English Witchcraft Act repeal of 1736 <citations>SP8C8xrQ:1-2,SP8C8xrQ:5</citations>.

## Part IV — The single apparatus

<claim id="c-2f9pxq" text="Nineteenth-century legal iconography and gendered codification are two registers of a single legal apparatus — the fetishized form of exchange visualised in allegory, and the explicit legal violence of expropriation statutorily codified — theorizable together through Gonçalves' two-sided reformulation of Pashukanis and Federici.">The Justitia on the courthouse pediment and the coverture provision on the civil-code page are not two topics but two faces of one apparatus. Pashukanis explains why the iconography must take the abstract, allegorical form it does: the fetishized commodity-form demands a fetishized visual form, and specific embodied women cannot occupy that place without breaking the formal-equality conceit. Federici explains why actual women had to be absent from the bench, the polling station, the guild, and the parish vestry at the same moment Justitia was being installed on the facade: the substrate the formal-equal subject stands on is produced and maintained by explicit legal violence exercised on women's bodies, labour, and reproductive capacity. Gonçalves' reformulation is what allows both statements to be true of one legal order rather than two.<citations>gj3SectA:1-3,gj3SectA:13,UFspHLhg:2-3,UFspHLhg:7,UFspHLhg:12</citations></claim>

The pairing lets one *date* both sides together. The period at which the codifications are most fiercely contested — Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882, CD Acts repeal of 1886, Brazilian Civil Code debates of 1901–1916 — is also the period at which Justitia is most heavily deployed on courthouse pediments and the Republic most heavily engraved on national coinage. The same iconographic tradition long used by the state to legitimise its authority could also be turned against state-sanctioned injustice: Collins shows American suffragists appropriating Justice, "a legible symbol of law's legitimacy," to declare the illegitimacy of laws passed by unrepresentative legislatures <citations>_QzobmPA:3-4,6</citations>.

## Part VI — Application to the ICONOCRACIA problem

For the ICONOCRACIA project — visual sovereignty in Latin America, Brazilian legal codification, and the political economy of state-formation — Gonçalves' formulation has three specific advantages.

*Latin-American originated.* Andermann's volume argues that in Latin America visual culture carried unusually heavy weight in producing nation-state consent — literacy was restricted, so the iconographic apparatus did more of the legitimation work — and that the iconography of the state-form "has so far escaped serious critical treatment" <citations>9T39Z-0w:0-2</citations>. Gonçalves' framework is designed to hold that iconographic apparatus and the codification programs (Brazilian Penal Code 1890; Civil Code 1916) within one analytic system.

*Avoids the false choice.* The framework does not ask whether legal form or gendered expropriation is primary; it asks how each side of accumulation operates in a given historical conjuncture and how they interlock — the correct question for a project whose object occupies both sides at once.

*Iconography theoretically continuous with codification.* The female allegorical body is not a supplementary cultural object to be explained by reference to legal history; it is the visible surface of the fetishized side of the very same legal order whose expropriative side is the statutory apparatus. Iconography and codification stand as two coordinates of one political-economic system.

## Part VII — Where the framework remains contested

Three real caveats.

The most orthodox Pashukanians (Wilén, Dimick) argue that form-analysis can be extended to gender and race without a Federician supplement — the commodity-form approach is more flexible than its feminist critics suppose <citations>bke1XFMQ:5-6</citations>. If they are right, Gonçalves' two-sided formulation collapses back into a single reconstructed Pashukanian theory.

Federici herself has been pressed on the theoretical status of patriarchy — whether her account effectively grants it transhistorical autonomy that is then captured by capital, the "origins-subsistence" reading Portella and Busk push back against <citations>No-WBz5w:0-1,No-WBz5w:3</citations>.

The nineteenth-century codification arc is not monotonic. The same Justitia icon that legitimised the codes was being repurposed against them by suffragists <citations>_QzobmPA:3-4,6</citations>; the Married Women's Property Acts and the CD Acts repeal are within the century. Any application of the framework that presents the two sides as smoothly interlocking will underdescribe the contestation that runs through both — a point that itself follows from the framework, since a two-sided order under strain will be contested on both sides.
