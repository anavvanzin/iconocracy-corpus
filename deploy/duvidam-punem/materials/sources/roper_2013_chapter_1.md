## Executive Summary
Lyndal Roper repositions Heinrich Kramer’s *Malleus Maleficarum* (1487) as a critical "event" in the history of gender and demonology rather than a simple technical manual of witch-hunting procedure. For Roper, the *Malleus* acts as an active **technology of gender** and a piece of **misogynistic pedagogy** directed at educating inquisitors on how to construct, interpret, and suspect the female body. Rather than reflecting an existing popular reality, the text discursively *produces* the subject of the "witch" by organizing a gendered regime of truth.

---

## Key Concepts and Theoretical Insights

### 1. The Malleus as an "Event" Rather than a Manual
- Historical scholarship in the 19th and early 20th centuries (e.g., Hansen, Michelet) often treated the *Malleus* as a neutral, technical handbook of procedural demonology.
- Roper rejects this static view. She argues that the publication of the *Malleus* was a discursive *event* that fundamentally reshaped early modern mentalities.
- It systematized disparate folklore, legal traditions, and theological arguments into a single, cohesive, and highly authoritative category of "the female witch" characterized by heretical demonolatry (specifically involving pacts and carnal relations with devils).

### 2. Technology of Gender and Misogynistic Pedagogy
- Roper frames the text as a pedagogical device meant to instruct male inquisitors in the active suspicion of female bodies.
- The *Malleus* teaches that female anatomy, emotionality, and intellect are inherently unstable and prone to demonic corruption.
- It provides a structural framework through which the inquisitor does not "discover" witches but rather *manufactures* them through highly guided interrogation, torture, and confession. The female body is treated as the "archive of truth" where the inquisitor reads the signs of heresy.

## Core Textual Citations and Excerpts

### Part I, Question Six (Questio VI)
The pivot of the entire treatise is Question Six of Part I: *"Quare in sexu famineo tanta reperiatur superstitio?"* ("Why is it that women are more prone to superstition?"). 

- **Etymological Construction:**
  The authors famously construct a false, misogynistic etymology for the word *femina* (woman) to prove their theological point:
  > *"femina enim dicitur a fe et minus, quia semper minorem habet et servat fidem"*  
  > (Translation: "For 'woman' is said to be derived from 'fe' [faith] and 'minus' [less], since she always has and keeps less faith.")

- **Citing Misogynistic Patristic and Classical Authorities:**
  Kramer and Sprenger compile a vast array of hostile citations, most notably John Chrysostom, to frame women as a necessary but dangerous social evil:
  > *"What else is woman but a foe to friendship, an unescapable punishment, a necessary evil, a natural temptation, a desirable calamity, a domestic danger, a delectable detriment, an evil of nature, painted with fair colours!"*

- **The Moral Ambivalency (Extreme Polarities):**
  The text claims that women lack moderation and exist only in moral extremes:
  > *"A woman either loves or hates; there is no third grade."*  
  > *"When a woman thinks alone, she thinks evil."*

- **The Source of Witchcraft in Carnal Lust:**
  Ultimately, the *Malleus* links the rise of witchcraft to female sexuality and insatiable carnal desire:
  > *"All witchcraft comes from carnal lust, which in women is insatiable..."*

## Methodological Utility for the Article
For the study of the Portuguese Inquisition and the case of Maria Gonçalves Cajada, Roper’s framework allows us to analyze:
1. **The stabilization of the type penal:** How the *Malleus* established a structural "pedagogy of suspicion" that was codified into the standard operating manuals of Iberian inquisitors.
2. **The "corpo confitente":** The inquisitorial procedure of torture and confession is read not as "finding" a witch, but as the procedural production of a confessant subject—writing the *Malleus*'s categories onto the physical flesh of the accused.
3. **The transition from Latin to the Vernacular:** How the academic, Latinate category of *maleficium* translates into the vernacular, popular imaginary of the colony (e.g., Cajada's nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo").
