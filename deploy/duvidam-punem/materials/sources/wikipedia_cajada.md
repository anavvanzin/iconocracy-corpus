## Encyclopedic Summary
Maria Gonçalves Cajada, popularly known by the pejorative nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" (Estremoz, Portugal — unknown date of death), is considered by historians (such as Laura de Mello e Souza) to be the most famous "witch" or *feiticeira* of 16th-century colonial Brazil. Her trial (Processo nº 10748 of the Inquisition of Lisbon) during the First Inquisitorial Visitation to Brazil (1591–1593) is one of the most thoroughly documented and studied cases of colonial magical practices.

## Core Biographical and Historical Details

### 1. Origins and First Conviction
- Born in Estremoz, Alentejo, Portugal.
- Classed as a *cristã-velha* (old Christian, of non-Jewish descent).
- Married to a Portuguese sailor, she spent most of her adult life in precarious socioeconomic conditions.
- Before being exiled to Brazil, she was arrested and convicted in Portugal for setting fire to several houses and physically assaulting a local judge.
- For these crimes, she performed a public penance wearing a *carocha* (infamous pointed hat) and was sentenced to six years of exile (*degredo*) to Pernambuco, Brazil.

### 2. Life in Salvador, Bahia (1578–1591)
- After serving her time or moving from Pernambuco, she settled in Salvador, Bahia, around 1578.
- For over twelve years, she operated as a professional practitioner of magic, love potions, healing, and divinations.
- She had no fixed address, frequently renting rooms from local women.
- Her clientele included prominent colonial figures (both men and women) who hired her to "amansar" (tame/subdue) violent husbands, cure illnesses, find lost items, or ensure love.
- Her expressive, transgressive, and independent lifestyle led her neighbors to coin the somatic, pejorative nickname "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" (indicating a volatile, hot-tempered, and sexually suspect personality).

### 3. Arrest and Trial during the First Visitation (1591)
- In 1591, Heitor Furtado de Mendonça arrived in Salvador to conduct the First Visitation of the Holy Office.
- Cajada’s neighbors denounced her systematically. Neighbors like Catarina Quaresma, however, offered dissonant voices, indicating that some in the community did not believe she was in league with the devil, but rather viewed her as a normal, helpful neighbor.
- Arrested by the visitador, she was held in strict secrecy. Her modest possessions (including some clothes and household goods) were confiscated.
- Shipped to Lisbon in chains, she was interrogated at the Rossio Inquisition Palace. Under torture, she confessed to making demonic pacts and invoking the devil.
- The trial ended with a verdict of continued exile and public penance. Notably, the judges registered their own institutional doubt about her crimes, noting: *"Parece que tudo são embustes e enganos as culpas desta ré."* ("It seems that all the charges of this defendant are but tricks and deceits.")

## Historiographical Significance
1. **The Translation of Magic:** The case illustrates how the Inquisition translated local, popular, and often transactional magical practices (such as amansar maridos) into a formal, theological heresy of devil-worship.
2. **The "Arde-lhe-o-rabo" Label:** The nickname serves as a perfect example of popular, vernacular labeling.
3. **Gender and Marginalization:** Cajada’s life represents the precarious survival strategies of exiled, poor Portuguese women in the colonies, who carved out spaces of agency through magical services, but were ultimately targeted by the joint machinery of the crown and Church.
