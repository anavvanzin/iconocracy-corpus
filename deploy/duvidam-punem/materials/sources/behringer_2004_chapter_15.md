## Executive Summary
Wolfgang Behringer’s structural global history of witchcraft treats the early modern witch-hunts as a core component in the rise of the modern, centralized state. In his writings on the Iberian Peninsula (Spain, Portugal, and their colonial networks), Behringer emphasizes that the Inquisition was fundamentally an agent of the state/crown rather than a purely ecclesiastical institution. For Behringer, the expansion of witch trials to the colonies was a structural tool of "social disciplining" used to establish metropolitan hegemony. Crucially, Behringer offers a three-layered analytical typology for studying witchcraft: **popular belief** (*crença popular*), **accusatory practice** (*prática acusatória*), and **doctrinal discourse** (*discurso doutrinário*).

---

## Key Analytical Frameworks

### 1. The Tripartite Typology of Witchcraft Analysis
Behringer suggests that witchcraft must be disassembled into three distinct layers to be analyzed accurately by the historian:
- **Doctrinal Discourse** (*Discurso Doutrinário*): The elite, theological, and legal treatises compiled in Latin (such as the *Malleus Maleficarum*). It represents the conceptualization of the "witch" as a demonic heretic.
- **Accusatory Practice** (*Prática Acusatória*): The actual, local legal procedures, depositions, interrogations, and torture sessions that occur when a trial is launched.
- **Popular Belief** (*Crença Popular*): The vernacular, pre-modern folklore of a community concerning magical healing, love magic, or protection.

This article adopts Behringer's typology to show that the *Malleus* operates at the level of *Doctrinal Discourse* (Seção 1), the Inquisition operates at the level of *Accusatory Practice* (Seção 2), and Cajada’s neighbors and clientele operate at the level of *Popular Belief* (Seção 3).

### 2. State-Building and Social Disciplining
- Behringer argues that the early modern state utilized the legal repression of deviance (heresy, sodomy, and witchcraft) to assert its sovereign authority over its citizens.
- In the Portuguese Empire, the Holy Office was a key bureaucratic vector of this "social disciplining." By policing private morals, domestic healing, and sexuality, the crown established deep control over both the domestic population and the newly colonized Atlantic territories.

### 3. Structural, Interconnected Transatlantic Circuits
- For Behringer, the witch-hunts in the colonies and the metropole were not separate, parallel events.
- They were constitutively connected: the first visitations to Bahia (1591–1593) were structurally linked to the political and intellectual anxieties of Lisbon. The circulation of suspects, manuals, and judicial decrees created a unified Atlantic space of judicial subjection.
