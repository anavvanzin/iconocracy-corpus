# Brazil — The Republic, the Enslaved, and the Maiden Who Could Not Figure the Universal

**Draft case study — companion to *The Coin and the Witch*** · Ana Vanzin · PPGD/UFSC · working document
**Register:** extension/companion to the Federician-iconography essay; not yet integrated. Honest caveats at the end.

---

## Abstract

The four figures that *The Coin and the Witch* resolves into one template — Marianne, Britannia, Justitia, Columbia — share a mechanism: the female civic sign becomes conscriptable for the universal exactly because concrete women have been evacuated, by force, of the political-economic standing that would tie the sign to a particular interest. This case extends the template to Brazil and argues that Brazil is not a peripheral illustration of it but a **sharpening of its racial logic**, in one specific way. In Federici's European register, the woman evacuated from the civic universal is the *peasant, the witch* — a gendered evacuation. In Brazil, the accumulation is doubly constitutive: post-slavery and settler-colonial. Above the economic mechanism, the case reads the same operation through Françoise Choay's *A alegoria do patrimônio* — heritage as an allegorical **mirror whose narcissistic contemplation appeases our anxieties** — to name the non-economic, symbolic-institutional layer by which the Republic *patrimonializes* its own white Maiden as the national self-image. The narcissistic comportment is itself a racial operation: the mirror the Republic institutes reflects only the white, classical, civic self. The white civic Maiden is installed precisely as the post-abolition black population is being made not merely a gendered-other but a **racial other**. The sharpening is Lélia Gonzalez's *racismo por denegação*: the black female body is celebrated in the register of devotion and erased in the register of public representation. Brazil therefore runs the template with **two female signs at once** — the white Maiden conscripted to the universal (the *Efígie da República* on the cédula, the classical goddess of Villares, Lopes Rodrigues, Chambelland, Visconti), and the black devotional Maiden (Nossa Senhora Aparecida) confined to the particular and to the chapel. The general equivalent is not merely masculine: it is white; and the black female is not even a secondary sign, she is the term the semiotic slot *excludes*. Brazil is the template's limit case, and the limit is racial.

## I. Why Brazil, and why here

The template has a logic, and the logic has a limit. Primitive accumulation produces a gendered subject-form — the female body enclosed, expropriated, converted into an unwaged reservoir of reproductive labour, and criminalised when it acts autonomously. That subject is then available for civic abstraction, because it has been emptied of the standing that would tie it to a particular interest. The coin is where the operation becomes visible: the masculine general equivalent — the sovereign, the citizen, the measure — stamps its authority on the female secondary sign. Warner names the pattern; Goux and Cady supply the semiotic architecture; Federici supplies the mechanism.

Columbia already pushes the template into the colonial register. The substitution her archive records — Indian Princess to Greek Goddess, dated 1783–1815 and refigured through the post-abolition banishment of black figures from her Temple of Liberty — is the moment the template stops being merely European. But Columbia is still a settler-colonial figure of a polity that *becomes* an imperial power. Brazil is a different shape: a settler-colonial and **post-slavery** polity whose imperial phase is exactly the one that is being repudiated in 1889, and whose founding moment as a Republic coincides with the making of a formally free but substantively excluded black population. Brazil, in other words, installs the white civic Maiden **at the same moment** it is putting the black post-emancipation population outside the civic universal. It is not a belated colonial echo. It is the template's sharpest test, because the evacuation that makes the female sign conscriptable is in Brazil *doubled* — by gender and by race — and the two are not separable.

That is the case this section makes, and it is why Brazil, not Columbia, is where the template should be pressed hardest.

## II. The acculturation does not end in 1888 — it restarts on the cédula

Abolition (13 May 1888) and the Republic (15 November 1889) are sixteen months apart. The coincidence is not a detail. The polity that declared the slaves free in 1888 declared itself a Republic in 1889, and in the same stroke began engraving its own image as a woman. The *Efígie da República* appears on the inaugural silver 1000 réis (1889, .917), on the 20.000 réis ouro with the Phrygian cap (1889–1922), on the 2000 réis and the 500 réis (1912), and into the twentieth century on the Cruzeiro cédulas. The figure is neoclassical, draped, and — as the genealogy of the thesis puts it — the toga and drape do not merely desexualise the body, they **classify it racially**: the Greco-Roman vestment situates the allegorical female in a white, European corporality.

The Phrygian cap is the tell. It is the attribute of the French *Liberté* — the grammar of the Revolution that had juxtaposed, in one window, the engraving of Marianne on the 1792 seal and the guillotining of Olympe de Gouges. Brazil imported that grammar wholesale. The barrete frígio on the Brazilian Republica marks a transatlantic transfer: the neoclassical female figure arrives in Brazil as a *ready, pre-emptied sign*. She was already the vessel for a universal-that-excludes, in France. Brazil acquaints her with the Republic's founding without rewritening her, and places her on the very instrument — the cédula — through which the same Republic circulates its value.

Read through the template, the cédula is not a neutral object that happens to bear a female face. It is the pairing made daily: the masculine measure of the sovereign on one side, the feminine matter to be measured on the other. In Brazil the pairing carries a further charge. The Republic that stamps the white Maiden on its money is the Republic that, in 1891, under the first Republican Constitution, withholds citizenship-civic equality from women — as the French Republic had in a comparable founding window — and is simultaneously managing the aftermath of abolition, the legality of which had been staged, two years before the Republic, through a *female* allegory: Visconti's *A Lei Áurea* (project for a Correios stamp, 1904). The abolition of slavery is personified as a woman, in a white neoclassical idiom. The emancipated — the people whose freedom that law declared — are not the figure who stands for the freedom.

## III. The two Maidens: Aparecida and the Republic

Brazil's sharpening is not that it has one conscripted Maiden. It is that it has **two female signs occupying opposite registers**, and the relation between them is the *racismo por denegação* that Gonzalez names: the symbolic spaces erase black presence precisely while they naturalise white symbolic presence.

On one side is the white civic Maiden — the classical goddess on the cédula, the seal, the medal, the frontispiece, the courthouse and the state building. Décio Villares's *Alegoria da República* (c. 1919) and his positivist ensemble; Manoel Lopes Rodrigues's *Alegoria da República* (1896); Carlos Chambelland's *Alegoria da República* (1922); Eliseu Visconti's *Alegoria* for the Biblioteca Nacional (1910) and his 1904 Correios stamp projects. These are the faces of the Republic, and they are structurally, archetypally white: the classical vestment situates them in a white European corporality, and they are conscripted to the universal — the Nation, the Law, the Republic — precisely so.

On the other side is the black devotional Maiden — Nossa Senhora Aparecida, the black Marian figure, fished from the Paraíba in 1717, darkened (by the river, by devotion), and solemnly crowned in 1904, the same year Visconti stages Abolition as a white female allegory. Aparecida is the *other* female figure of Brazil, and she is not conscripted to the universal. She is conscripted to the particular and the private — the chapel, the shrine, the votive offenda, the pilgrimage, the devotio. Gonzalez's insight is that this is not benign. The black female body is celebrated in devotion — she is the object of the most intense popular veneration in Brazil — and she is erased from public representation. The white Republic is public and sovereign; the black Aparecida is private and devotional.

Read through the template, the pair is exact. The white Maiden is the secondary sign conscripted to the general equivalent: she figures the universal while the universal is being withheld. The black Maiden is not a secondary sign at all — she is the term the semiotic slot *excludes*. That is the sharpening. In Marianne's France, one female sign is conscripted and the concrete woman is evacuated: a single register. In Brazil there are two female signs, and the operation splits: the white one is conscripted to the universal, the black one is confined to the particular, and the *denegação* is the mechanism by which the black body can be venerated without ever being a face of the state. The white civic Maiden and the black devotional Maiden are the two halves of one operation — the first installs the universal, the second absorbs the particular, and the state's image stays white.

## IV. The general equivalent, whitened

The Goux/Cady pairing is gendered: the general equivalent is masculine, the secondary sign is feminine. Brazil forces the pairing to admit a third term it has been hiding: the general equivalent is not merely masculine, it is **white**. The measure that stamps authority — the sovereign, the citizen, the property-holder, the free worker — is not just active and male, it is white. The secondary sign conscripted to the universal is not just passive and female, it is **white-feminine**. And the black female is excluded from the pairing entirely: neither measure nor matter, but the body whose own freedom the Republic's female allegory stages in the abstract while the concrete subject of that freedom remains outside the civic universal.

This is the racial content of the sign that Columbia's case had already begun to expose and Brazil makes unmissable. The "universal" classical form is a whitening operation: the Greco-Roman drape erases the specificity of the body it clothes and, in so doing, places the allegorical female in a European corporality that has nothing to do with the bodies who actually laboured on Brazilian soil. The *Contrato Racial Visual* — the thesis's own concept for the constitutive whiteness of the "universal" allegory, and the transatlantic transfer of neoclassical models — names precisely this. Brazil's Republic does not merely borrow the European grammar; it borrows it already racialised, and installs it over a population whose blackness the universal cannot carry. The figure of the *dispossessed* — the enslaved, the formerly enslaved, the black woman — is retired from public representation at exactly the moment the Republic needs a face, and that face is white.

## V. The patrimonial mirror: Choay and the non-economic mechanism

The economic mechanism is necessary but not sufficient. Federici explains *why the female sign was available*; she does not explain *why the new regime began to designate that sign as its own heritage, its own monument, to be conserved and celebrated as the image of the nation*. That second, symbolic-institutional operation is Françoise Choay's object, and it is the layer the template has been missing. It is not economic — it does not run through accumulation or expropriation — and it is what turns the civic Maiden from the *output* of a process into the *object of a patrimonial narcissism*.

Choay's distinction between the **monument** and the **monument historique** is the load-bearing distinction here. The *monument* (from *monere*, to remind) is a work deliberately erected to make a past or an event felt — meaning-bearing, addressed to affectivity, designed to gather a community around its memory. The *monument historique* is a different thing: an object whose value is not that it was built to remember, but that it has been *designated* by an institution — the historian, the society, the state — as historically significant. Any object can become one; and the category is born, as Choay shows, with the French Revolution's destructions and the great inventory, when a society begins to conserve objects because they have been *chosen*. The civic Maiden is both at once. She is a monument in the first sense: the Republic erects her precisely to gather the new nation around a manufactured founding memory. And she is a monument historique in the second: the Republic, in the same stroke, *designates* her its own history, its own patrimony — she goes on the cédula, on the seal, on the state building, and is conserved as the face of the nation she is meant to have founded.

That designation is where the narcissism enters. Choay's *A alegoria do patrimônio* is literally titled for it: heritage functions as an **allegory**, a sign that stands for something by a code — the book's own synopsis names it a "espelho cuja contemplação narcisista aplaca nossas angústias," a mirror whose narcissistic contemplation appeases our anxieties. The heritage institution, on her account, is a self-representation of the present: we conserve not to honour the past in its alterity, nor to build for the future, but to see *ourselves* reflected in the objects we preserve. The patrimonialized object is a mirror, and the comportment toward it is narcissistic — a self-recognition propped up as a relation to history.

Apply this to the Republic and the sharpening becomes exact. The white civic Maiden is not merely engrained; she is **patrimonialized** — designated as the national heritage, the monument the Republic conserves as its own image. And the mirror is white. The narcissistic comportment is the Republic looking at its own just-minted Maiden and recognising the nation it wants to be: classical, civic, universal, white. It is a *double* allegory, and the stacking matters. The thesis's own *alegoria feminina* is an allegory — a meaning-bearing sign prescribed by a code (the venda, the balança, the barrete). Choay's *alegoria do patrimônio* is another, at one level up: the state **allegorizes its own allegory**. It takes its civic Maiden — a sign for the Republic — and designates it as the Republic's heritage, its self-image, the thing it venerates and consigns to the museum. That second allegorization is the mechanism by which the sign is *frozen as value*, detached from the history that produced it, and held up for self-contemplation.

And it is a racial operation, which is precisely the point of adding Choay above Federici rather than instead of her. Choay's narcissism tells us *why* the state conserves its own image; the racial content tells us *whose* image. The mirror the Republic institutes reflects only the white, classical, civic self — because that is the self the Republic wants to see. The black population is not in the mirror. Nossa Senhora Aparecida is not patrimonialized as the civic Maiden is; she is *venerated*, in the chapel, the shrine, the procession — the register of devotion, not of representation. The "comportamento narcísico" of the heritage and the "racismo por denegação" of Gonzalez converge: the narcissistic self-contemplation of the Republic in its white Maiden *is* the denial of the black body. The state looks at its own white image and is appeased; the black body is the term the mirror will not reflect. Choay names the mirror; Gonzalez names what the mirror refuses to show.

## VI. The counter-operation: what the template's limit exposes

The template has a limit, and Brazil is where it surfaces not as a caveat but as a feature of the object. A hard question follows: if the black female is excluded from the semiotic slot, does she return as a *contra-sign*? The thesis's own apparatus says yes — in the register of **contra-alegoria**, the figure that subverts rather than reproduces the iconocracy. The Mexican *Paseo de la Reforma* case is the reverse: a masculine colonial figure refused, a female allegory of justice installed in its place. And the Brazilian counter-operation is the *antropofagia* / Abaporu case — a female-ambiguous allegory that derives its meaning not from the universal but from devouring it (Oswald's *Manifesto Antropófago*, 1928, contemporaneous with Benjamin's *Ursprung des deutschen Trauerspiels*, the work that reclaims allegory against the symbol).

The point for the Brazilian extension is not to fold these into the template. It is to say what the template cannot: the white civic Maiden's authority in Brazil is contested *from below* by a population whose blackness the universal will not carry, and that contestation has its own iconography — the devotional, the devotional-amorous, the anti-academic, the anthropophagic. Brazil's sharpening is therefore both a hardening (a whitening) and a disclosure: the racial logic of the general equivalent is visible on the cédula, and it is the logic that a counter-iconography — from Aparecida's chapel to Abaporu's foot — works to tear. The template explains the Maiden; the limit explains the counter-Maiden.

## VII. Caveats and pressure points

The extension inherits the two central exposures of *The Coin and the Witch* and adds one of its own.

| Pressure | Honest account |
|---|---|
| **The transmission claim** | Federici's window is 15th–17th-century Europe; Brazil's civic Maiden consolidates in 1889–1930, centuries later. The argument cannot claim contemporaneity. It runs through a transmission claim: the gendered-and-racialised subject forged in accumulation became the raw material later states drew on. Plausible, and not unprecedented (Doy makes a version for revolutionary France); but a claim about transmission, not a demonstrated causal chain. |
| **The racial extension** | Federici's *Caliban* is set in England and Europe; the extension into post-slavery, settler-colonial (and the *postcolonial* Global South her later work gestures at) is genuinely a widening of her object, not a straightforward application. Careful critics will press this exactly. |
| **The primary/secondary divide** | Much of the Federici material is reception rather than primary text, and the Brazilian side leans on Gonzalez (1984), Carvalho (1990), Schwarcz, and Nascimento & Batista (2022). A publication version must close the loop with the primary texts. |
| **From state iconography to heritage** | Choay's argument is built on *architectural and urban* heritage — the conservation of buildings, monuments, and historic urban fabric — not on state iconography, coin, or the female allegory. Reading the civic Maiden through her *patrimonial mirror* is an extension of her concept across media, not an application of it. The narcissism she names is homologous to the denegação; the two are not the same mechanism, and pressing them into identity would over-reach. |
| **The flattening risk** | Brazil is a specific formation — a post-slavery, settler-colonial, Catholic Marian polity — not merely a colonial illustration of a European template. Pressing the family resemblance too hard would reproduce the very whitening the argument diagnoses. The analytic weight of Brazil's specificity has to be carried case by case. |
| **The gap claim** | The claim that no one has staged the Warner–Federici comparison on civic female allegory rests on a bounded search, and the Brazilian historiographical tradition (Carvalho, Schwarcz, Gonzalez, Nascimento & Batista) documents the substitution of the Indian/black figure by the white classical goddess *without* routing it through Federici. The synthesis is the case study's; the substitution is theirs. |

None of these undoes the reading. Each names the work a published version would have to do to turn the proposal into a proof. What the Brazilian case establishes is not that Federici's mechanism applies to Brazil, but that the template's racial limit — the whitening of the general equivalent — is where the mechanism is most legible, and where the counter-allegory is most alive.
