## Abstract
This paper analyzes the historical and structural mechanisms of the "positivist freeze" in Brazilian legal iconography. Drawing from a canonical corpus of official state devices (coins, banknotes, stamps, and judicial monuments), we investigate why the modern state persistently adopts the female form to personify its highest sovereign values while excluding actual women from the exercise of political and judicial power. By deploying a sequence of quantitative metrics (the 10 indicators of symbolic purification and Kruskal-Wallis tests) alongside qualitative iconology, we contrast the postural rigidity of Brazilian forensical allegories with the dynamic, rotating iconographic regimes of France and Germany. We demonstrate that the petrification of the female allegory in Brazil—embodied in Alfredo Ceschiatti’s *A Justiça* (1961) and the unyielding Republican Effigy on national currency—constitutes a visiocratic technology of institutional stabilization. This "visual sexual contract" offers symbolic visibility as a substitute for civic reciprocity, a silent pact that was materially fractured during the iconoclastic events of January 8, 2023.

## Section 1 — Introduction: The Face of the Law and the Silent Contract (Ceschiatti and the STF)

In the middle of the Praça dos Três Poderes in Brasília, directly in front of the glass monument designed by Oscar Niemeyer to house the Supreme Federal Tribunal (STF), sits Alfredo Ceschiatti’s monumental sculpture *A Justiça* (1961) [BR-009](file:///Users/ana/Research/hub/iconocracy-corpus/corpus/corpus-data.json#L2347). Carved from a single, heavy block of grey Petrópolis granite, the figure is a seated woman. She is blindfolded, but her blindfold is not a textile wrap tied behind her head in the classical European tradition; it is a solid, continuous extension of the granite itself, an anatomical absence of eyes carved directly into the stone. On her lap, she holds a sword with both hands. Her body is rigid, blocky, almost fused to the monolithic chair beneath her. She exhibits no dynamic tension, no muscular movement, and no narrative agency. She is the lithic face of the Law in the capital of Brazilian modernism.

The presence of this feminine allegory at the heart of Brazil’s judicial sovereignty presents a historical and political paradox. At the moment of the sculpture’s installation in 1961—marking the geopolitical transfer of the capital to the central plateau under President Juscelino Kubitschek’s developmentalist promises—actual Brazilian women remained largely excluded from the higher echelons of the state. It was only in 2000, nearly forty years after Ceschiatti finished carving his granite Justice and more than a century after the Proclamação da República (1889), that the first woman, Minister Ellen Gracie, was appointed to a seat on the Supreme Federal Tribunal. For generations of legal history, the state chose a female form to represent the administration of justice while simultaneously denying real women the authority to judge.

This paradox is the core symptom of what we define as the **Visual Sexual Contract** (*Contrato Sexual Visual*), a concept that builds upon Carole Pateman’s (1988) classic critique of the social contract. While Pateman demonstrated that the political contract establishing modern civil society is underpinned by a patriarchal pact over women's bodies, she left the visual and symbolic dimensions of this power structure largely unexamined. Modern states did not merely silence women in their constitutions and codes; they hyper-visualized them in their public spaces, currencies, and official seals. By transforming the female body into an empty, desexualized, and allegorical container for state virtues—a process we term **Feminilidade de Estado** (State Femininity)—the legal culture created a visiocratic mechanism of legitimacy. This mechanism offers symbolic recognition in place of civic reciprocity: the female figure is granted the status of an absolute signifier of the state on the condition that real women are denied political subjecthood.

In the case of Brazil, this visual sexual contract did not follow the fluid, fluctuating patterns of metropolitan Europe. Rather, it resulted in a **"positivist freeze"** (*congelamento positivista*). In nineteenth-century France, for instance, the allegorical representation of the Republic (Marianne) shifted dynamically depending on the political regime: from the revolutionary, bare-breasted, armed woman of 1848 to the domestic, seated, and peaceful *Type Sage* stamp of the Third Republic. In contrast, the Brazilian Republic adopted a rigid, highly purified, and static version of the classical effigy in 1889 [8145d1a4-3cbd-422c-937f-9ce302084f0c](file:///Users/ana/Research/hub/iconocracy-corpus/corpus/corpus-data.json#L1377). Heavily influenced by the Positivist church and the military elite, the new regime immediately petrified the image of the Republic in its inaugural coinage and stamps, freezing the allegorical body to lock out any revolutionary or popular excess. Ceschiatti's *A Justiça* is the mid-twentieth-century architectural crystallization of this long-term trend: a modernism that relies on the ancient, unmoving vocabulary of classical virtues to secure institutional stability.

For over sixty years, Ceschiatti’s sculpture stood as the undisturbed visual anchor of this quiet contract. However, on January 8, 2023, the lithic silence of the allegory was violently broken. During the attacks on the headquarters of the Three Powers in Brasília, rioters vandalized the monument, spray-painting the phrase *"perdeu, mané"* (a colloquial phrase meaning "you lost, fool," referencing a statement by STF Minister Luís Roberto Barroso) across the granite lap of *A Justiça*. This act of iconoclasm was not a simple defacement of public property; it was a material rupture of the visual sexual contract itself. By scribbling the raw, informal, and masculine vernacular of political conflict onto the purified, desexualized body of the state allegory, the vandals dragged the silent symbol back into the violent arena of historical reality. The pichação revealed the fragility of the visiocratic consensus: when the real institutions of the state are contested, their stone women can no longer absorb the shocks of the political contract.

This paper uses this rupture as a starting point to unpack the genealogy of Virtues and the dynamics of the positivist freeze in Brazil. Through a quantitative analysis of 278 official devices coded with ten indicators of symbolic purification (including postural rigidity, desexualization, and architectural framing), we demonstrate how the Brazilian state systematically maximized the purification of its allegories to compensate for the instability of its democratic contracts. By tracing these patterns, we hope to contribute to a deeper visual literacy in legal history, showing that the history of law is written not only in the ink of its codes, but also in the stone and metal of its sovereign images.

## Section 2 — Theoretical Framework: Iconocracy, Visiocracy, and the Visual Sexual Contract (Pateman & Goodrich)

To comprehend the political work performed by Alfredo Ceschiatti’s granite *Justiça* or the Republican Effigy printed on fiat currency, we must build a bridge between two bodies of critical theory that have historically developed in isolation: the feminist critique of the political contract and the visiocratic critique of legal history. 

In her foundational work *The Sexual Contract*, Carole Pateman (1988) dismantled the liberal myth of the social contract. Pateman argued that the civil freedom of the modern citizen is not a universal category; it was established through a patriarchal pact that subordinated women to men. The social contract, therefore, is an illusion that conceals a primary *sexual contract*, which divides the political space into a public, civil sphere populated by masculine individuals and a private, domestic sphere where women are subjected. The modern state is built upon the exclusion of women’s political subjecthood. However, Pateman’s analysis is primarily textual and institutional; it details how the law excludes women through marriage contracts, labor relations, and constitutional architecture, but it ignores the visual landscape of sovereignty. Pateman does not explain why this patriarchal state, which excluded women from courts and parliaments, required the public squares and national currencies to be populated by giant, draped female bodies.

To solve this visual paradox, we must turn to Peter Goodrich’s (1995, 2014) concept of **visiocracy** and his recovery of the history of legal emblems. Goodrich reminds us that the authority of law is not merely a cognitive affair of texts, statutes, and logical inference; it is a system of images (*imago juris*) that commands emotional attachment and belief. The law governs the soul before it governs behavior, and it does so by capturing the imagination. The history of Western law, from the classical Roman *ius imaginum* to the baroque emblems, shows that the state requires a visible face to render its abstract authority tangible. 

We propose that the intersection of Pateman’s sexual contract and Goodrich’s visiocracy constitutes the **Visual Sexual Contract** (*Contrato Sexual Visual*). The modern state resolves the tension of excluding women from rights by granting them an absolute, purified visibility in the realm of representation. The allegorical female body is deployed as a visual technology of sovereignty: it functions as a "non-place" and an empty signifier. Precisely because the allegory is classical, desexualized, and atonic, it represents no actual woman. It belongs to no class, race, or family; it is a purified vessel through which the state projects its imaginary neutrality, justice, and liberty. This is what we define as **Feminilidade de Estado** (State Femininity). The state visualizes the feminine in proportion to its political exclusion: the allegory is a substitute, a ghost that occupies the civic space so that real women cannot.

This visiocratic economy operates through what Marie-José Mondzain (2002, 2005) calls the *iconic economy*—a management of the visible designed to secure institutional belief. The regime of images is not static; it adjusts to the political crises of the state. We identify three distinct **iconocratic regimes** (*regimes iconocráticos*) that organize State Femininity:

1. **The Foundational Regime (*Regime Fundacional*):** This regime operates at the moment of political rupture (revolutions, independence, new constitutions). Here, the female allegory is dynamic, revolutionary, and maternal; she is the "midwife" of a new order. Like Eugène Delacroix's *Liberty Leading the People* (1830), she is often active, bare-breasted, and walks through the debris of the old world. Her body represents the raw energy of the constituent power.
2. **The Normative Regime (*Regime Normativo*):** Once the new state stabilizes, the revolutionary woman must be domesticated. The constituent power transitions to the constituted power. The allegory is tamed, seated, draped, and serialized on daily objects like stamps, coins, and court seals. This is the regime of the *Type Sage* (1876) or the standard Republican Effigy in Brazil. The body is purified of all movement, securing the routine legitimacy of the bureaucratic state.
3. **The Military Regime (*Regime Militar*):** In moments of external threat or internal authoritarian turn, the allegory undergoes a physical hardening (*endurecimento*). The female body is covered in armor, helmeted, and armed with swords and shields. She becomes a fortress, calling the citizens to defend the boundaries of the state. Her posture becomes rigid and postural, reflecting the militarization of the sovereign contract.

In post-colonial contexts, this iconic economy is complicated by the transmission of the European classical canon to territories marked by racial and colonial hierarchies. When the Brazilian Republic imports the neoclassical, white Marianne to represent its new order in 1889, it is not merely importing a symbol of liberty; it is importing a **Visual Racial Contract** (*Contrato Racial Visual*) that establishes whiteness as the aesthetic threshold of civilization. The genealogy of classical Virtues—the *Nachleben* (afterlife) of Ripa's (1603) *Iconologia*—is redeployed in the tropics as an ideological tool to erase the indigenous and African bodies of the nation, freezing the allegory in an unyielding, classical stasis to secure the power of a post-colonial elite.
