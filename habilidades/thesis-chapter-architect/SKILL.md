# Thesis Chapter Architect

Make each chapter perform a necessary argumentative job. Preserve the author's voice while exposing weak claims, missing evidence, ornamental theory, and sections that do not advance the thesis.

## Workflow

1. Establish the chapter's role in the thesis:
   - central claim;
   - question answered;
   - dependency on earlier chapters;
   - contribution to later chapters;
   - evidence introduced here and nowhere else.
2. Inventory the supplied materials: draft prose, notes, images, object records, legal sources, bibliography, and unresolved questions.
3. Extract the actual argument rather than the announced topic. If the draft has several competing claims, rank them.
4. Build an argument map using `references/argument-map.md`.
5. Search external bibliography when authorized or required. Follow `references/bibliography-protocol.md` and compare rather than merely accumulate citations.
6. Identify the strongest counterargument, rival explanation, and disciplinary objection.
7. Design a chapter structure in which every section has a function, evidence base, risk, and transition.
8. Test conceptual consistency across terms such as iconocracy, sovereignty, representation, allegory, state, republic, justice, authority, gender, citizenship, and violence.
9. Distinguish:
   - claim;
   - evidence;
   - example;
   - context;
   - theoretical instrument;
   - speculation.
10. Produce a revision sequence ordered by argumentative leverage, not by ease.
11. Draft or rewrite prose only when requested. Match the author's established voice and cite sources precisely.

## Default output

# Chapter architecture: [working title]

## Diagnostic verdict
Give a direct assessment of what the chapter currently does, what it thinks it does, and the gap between them.

## One-sentence claim
Write the strongest defensible version of the chapter claim.

## Role in the thesis
Explain why this chapter must exist and what would be lost without it.

## Argument map
List premises, evidence, warrants, counterarguments, and conclusion.

## Proposed structure
For each section include:
- working title;
- purpose;
- claim;
- evidence or objects;
- external bibliography to engage;
- risk or likely objection;
- transition to the next section;
- approximate proportion of the chapter.

## Bibliography comparison
Group external works by agreement, tension, method, omission, and potential misuse. State what each source changes in the chapter.

## Missing evidence
Separate necessary evidence from desirable enrichment.

## Revision order
Give the next three to seven moves in priority order.

## Conceptual consistency audit
List unstable terms, silent shifts in meaning, anachronisms, and categories that require definition.

## External bibliography protocol

- Prefer primary sources, peer-reviewed scholarship, academic books, official archives, and authoritative legal materials.
- Search in Portuguese and relevant foreign languages.
- Compare publication date with the date of the claim or event.
- Verify titles, authors, editions, page numbers, and stable identifiers before citing.
- Do not cite a source merely because its keywords resemble the chapter.
- Explain whether a source supports, qualifies, contradicts, or reframes the argument.
- When current law, scholarship, or institutional information matters, browse and cite current sources.

## Writing rules

- Preserve productive complexity; remove decorative obscurity.
- Do not turn every section into a literature review.
- Do not use images as illustrations after the argument is already complete. Let visual evidence do analytic work.
- Avoid announcing grand stakes that the evidence cannot carry.
- Never fabricate quotations, page references, archival identifiers, or bibliographic details.
- Mark every unresolved citation or factual claim explicitly.
- Use the language requested by the user.
